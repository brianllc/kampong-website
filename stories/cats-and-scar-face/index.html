Kindergarten · ~1987

Cats and Scar Face

In the evenings there were cats.

They came out around the block when the heat went out of the day, sitting at the void decks or near the benches at the playground, washing themselves and watching everybody with their eyes half shut. Nobody owned them. Brian knew every one of them by sight, and some of them were beautiful.

There was a grey one with white feet that he liked best of all.

"I want a cat," he told Annie, one evening at the playground.

"Then ask Mum."

"I asked already."

"What did she say?"

"She said no."

"Then that is that," said Annie, who was older and knew how these things went.

They sat on the swings a while and watched the grey one wash its face under the bench.

Then Annie said, "You know what. If you catch one and bring it upstairs, then it is already at home. They cannot exactly throw it back down."

Brian considered this. It seemed, to a boy of five, like an extremely good plan. It also came from his big sister, which made it better still, because Annie was seven and knew more.

"You catch it," he said.

"You catch it. It's your cat."

So Brian went and caught it.

He went slowly at first, the way you are supposed to, one small step and then another, with his hand out low and his fingers rubbing together as if he had food. The grey cat watched him come. It let him get quite close, close enough that he could see the dust on its whiskers, and Brian was already thinking about where it would sleep.

Then he lunged.

The cat made one quick jump away, and Brian went after it. Across the grass, round the playground, back towards the block. He was quicker than he looked, and somewhere along the footpath he got both hands around it, and it twisted, and he held on with everything he had, because letting go had not once occurred to him.

The cat told him to let go in the only way it had.

It happened too fast to see. There was a movement by his face and a hot line across his cheek, and then the cat was gone, and Brian was standing by himself in the void deck, not really hurt, not really anything, faintly surprised.

Then he felt something wet on the left side of his face.

He put his fingers up to it, and looked at them, and they were red.

"Jiejie," he said. "Jiejie!"

She came round the pillar and stopped dead, and the look on her face frightened him far more than the blood had. She did not say a single word about the cat. She took his wrist and pulled him to the lift and kept looking sideways at his cheek all the way up, and by the time the doors opened she was already calling for Mum.

Mum sat him on the edge of the bath and cleaned it with something that stung a great deal more than the cat had, holding his chin steady with two fingers while he squirmed.

"Hold still," she said. "How did this happen?"

Brian did not have to answer. Annie was standing in the doorway, and Annie answered, and then Annie got the scolding of her life, right there in the bathroom, while Brian sat on the edge of the bath with a plaster going onto his cheek and felt, on the whole, that things had gone quite well for him.

The scratch scabbed over and itched and healed the way scratches do.

But it left a small scar on the left side of his face, near the ear, and it never entirely went away. He never did get a cat. He got the mark of one instead, and he has it still.

Upper Primary

Two Surnames

<!-- status: awaiting read · level: P4 · year: 1992 -->

Brian's form teacher in Primary Four was called Mrs Rena Oh-Tan.

He thought about this more than he thought about most things that year. Every other teacher he had ever had managed perfectly well with one surname. Mrs Koh was Mrs Koh. Ms Liew had been Ms Liew. Zhuo Laoshi was Zhuo Laoshi and nothing else. But Mrs Oh-Tan had two, joined in the middle by a small line, as though at some point she had been asked to choose and had decided to keep both.

That was the year Mrs Oh-Tan handed out something she said was important, and told them to give it to their parents when they got home.

Dad and Mum read it at the kitchen table. Something was changing. At the end of Primary Four, the whole level would be sorted into three groups, and which group you went into would follow you all the way to Primary Six.

It was the first year it was going to happen, so it had not been like this when Annie was in Primary Four. And because nobody had been through it before, the letter asked the parents to come in and have it explained.

Mum came on a weekday afternoon, in the good blouse she wore for things like this. Brian sat beside her on a chair meant for somebody smaller, in his own classroom, which felt entirely wrong with a parent in it. Mrs Oh-Tan sat on the other side of the desk with a folder in front of her.

"So," Mrs Oh-Tan said. "There are three."

She wrote them on a piece of paper, turned it round, and pushed it across so Mum could see.

"EM1, the children take English and mother tongue both at the higher level. EM2, English at the higher level and mother tongue at the ordinary one. That is where most of them go." She paused. "EM3, the mother tongue is at the basic level. Reading, listening, some conversation. That is all."

"And after Primary Six?" said Mum.

"It follows them," said Mrs Oh-Tan.

Mum nodded slowly and did not say anything for a moment, and Brian looked at the piece of paper upside down and thought that EM3 was a perfectly ordinary-looking thing to have written on a piece of paper.

Then Mrs Oh-Tan opened the folder.

"Now. Brian."

He sat up.

"He is a nice boy," she said. "I have no complaints about his behaviour at all. But his results are not strong. Across most of the subjects they are, I will be honest with you, lacklustre." She turned a page and looked at it rather than at either of them. "And his Chinese is very weak."

"I know," said Mum. "At home we speak English. He gets no practice with it outside the classroom."

"It does not change what happens in December," said Mrs Oh-Tan. She did not soften it.

"No," said Mum.

"If he carries on the way he is going," said Mrs Oh-Tan, "he will most likely be put into EM3."

She let that sit for a moment, and then she said the rest of it.

"And you should understand where EM3 goes, Mrs Lim. It does not stop at Primary Six. A child who comes out of EM3 will very likely be in the Normal stream in secondary school, not Express. That is the road it puts him on."

Brian waited to find out how frightened he was supposed to be. He looked at the folder, and at the piece of paper with the three groups on it, and he could not make himself feel anything much at all. It was April. December was a very long way away, and he was nine, and December had never once arrived when he expected it to.

So he turned and looked at his mother instead, to see how she was taking it.

Mum was sitting very straight with her handbag in her lap and both hands on the clasp. She was still looking at the piece of paper. And between her eyebrows two small lines had come up that Brian had never seen before.

He had seen her cross. He had seen her tired, and busy, and out of patience with all three of them at once. He knew all of those faces and could have drawn them. This was not one of them.

Mrs Oh-Tan was still talking. Mum was saying yes, and thank you, and asking what he should be doing at home, and writing something on the back of the letter. Brian heard almost none of it. He sat on the small chair in his own classroom and looked at the two lines between his mother's eyebrows.

They walked out to the bus stop together afterwards. It was the hot part of the afternoon and the tarmac gave the heat straight back up at them, and Mum did not say anything about the meeting at all.

Brian looked up at her, and then away, and then up again. The two lines were still there.

"I will study," he thought. "I will study harder, and I will get better at Chinese, so that Mum won't be that worried."

He did not say it out loud. It did not seem like the sort of thing you said out loud, and anyway he was not sure he could have explained which part of it he meant. But he stood there at the bus stop in the heat, and decided that would be what he would do.

Lower Primary · 1989

Summer Snow

Down one long side of the school field ran a row of Angsana trees. Some of them were enormous, twenty or thirty metres high, or so they looked to a boy who was one of the shortest in his class, and on a hot day their wide branches threw down a deep, cool shade.

And the days were nearly always hot. At recess the whole class would pour out onto the field to play catching, tearing across the grass in the scorching sun until their shirts stuck to their backs. When it grew too hot to bear, they would dash for the Angsana trees and stand panting in the shade, then charge out again the moment someone was brave enough.

One afternoon the trees were in full bloom. Every Angsana along the field had turned bright yellow, covered from branch to branch in tiny flowers, so that the whole row blazed gold against the sky. Brian had never seen them so bright.

"Wah, so many flowers," said Wei Hao, squinting up at them. "The trees all wearing golden crowns."

"Come on," said Brian. "You're 'it'."

And they were off across the field again, the whole pack of them, shrieking and dodging in the heat.

Then the wind came.

It came in one long strong gust across the field, bending the lalang at the edges and bowing the great Angsana branches. And out of the trees, all at once, came a rain of tiny things, floating and turning as they fell. For a moment Brian thought it was really raining. Then he saw that the whole air was full of yellow.

It was the Angsana flowers. The wind had shaken them loose, thousands upon thousands of them, and now they came drifting down out of the sky, slow and light, swirling on the breeze and settling everywhere. It was snow, Brian thought, exactly like the snow he had only ever seen in picture books and on Christmas cards, except that this snow was warm, and gold, and falling in the middle of a Singapore afternoon.

The game stopped. Nobody called catching any more. The children stood with their faces tipped up and their arms thrown out, turning slowly under the falling flowers and trying to catch them as they came.

"Summer snow!" shouted Wei Hao, spinning with his mouth open.

By the time the wind died, the whole field had changed. Where there had been green grass and brown earth there was now a soft yellow blanket, petals as far as Brian could see, laid down in a single afternoon as though somebody had quietly covered the earth while the children played.

The recess bell would ring soon, and they would have to file back inside and shake the yellow out of their hair and their collars. The next year the school would move to its new compound, and Brian would never see the summer snow fall like that again. But nobody knew that yet, and for now nobody moved. They simply stood in the middle of the gold field, in the warm yellow snow, and looked.

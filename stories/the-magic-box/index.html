Kindergarten · 1987

The Magic Box

Teck Whye Lane ran along a little hill, and Block 15 stood at the top of it, and that was where Brian lived.

Right in front of the block was the playground, which was the centre of everything. It was also in a straight line from the living room window, so that when it was time for dinner Mum did not have to come down. She leaned out and hollered, and the sound came right across the playground, and every child there stopped and turned to see whose mother it was.

"Brian! Come up and eat!"

"Coming!" Brian would shout, without moving at all.

To the left of the playground was the dirt patch, where the marbles were fought over in the evenings. To the right was the grass, and the trees.

The jackfruit trees were the tall ones. They went up and spread out over the walkway so you passed through their shade on the way to anywhere, and hanging up in them were those great lumpy green fruits, bigger than a boy's head and far too heavy for the branches they hung from.

Past the jackfruit was a whole patch of starfruit trees.

It was Yongjie who worked out what starfruit were really for. He came running up the slope one afternoon with one in each hand and a penknife he was not supposed to have.

"Eh, Brian. Look."

He put a starfruit on the step and cut it straight across the middle, and held up the piece.

It was a star. Not roughly a star. A proper five-pointed star, the kind you would draw yourself, except that nobody had drawn it, it had simply grown that way inside the fruit.

"Wah," said Brian.

"Got paint or not?"

They spent the rest of that afternoon stamping. Press the cut end into the paint, press it onto the paper, lift it off, and there was a star. Then another, and another, until the whole sheet was covered and their fingers were green and sticky and the starfruit had gone soft and useless.

Eating them was less of a success. When the fruit went ripe it dropped, and there were always some lying about in the grass, and every so often somebody decided that this time they would be sweet.

"This one looks sweet," Yongjie said, holding one up.

It was not sweet. It was watery and sour and faintly disappointing, and they agreed there and then never to bother again, and a few weeks later somebody tried it again anyway.

But before any of that, before the fruit came at all, the trees flowered. And when the trees flowered, the butterflies came.

They came in numbers Brian never saw anywhere else, drifting from tree to tree all through the patch, tumbling over one another in the sun, never in a hurry and never still. You could stand in the middle of them and turn round slowly and there would be butterflies on every side of you.

Then one Sunday, Dad came home with two butterfly nets.

Brian had not asked for them. That was the best part. Dad simply came in with two long-handled nets and held one out to him and one out to his big sister Annie.

"I saw these," Dad said. "I thought of your trees."

They took a biscuit tin with them for the butterflies and went down to the starfruit patch, and it turned out to be much harder than it looked. A butterfly does not fly in a line. It goes up when you swing low and sideways when you are certain of it, and Brian's first dozen swings caught nothing at all but warm air.

"You are chasing," said Annie, who was older and had already caught two. "Don't chase. Stand still and let it come, then go up from underneath."

"That is what I am doing."

"That is not what you are doing."

Brian stood still. His arms ached from holding the net ready. A small yellow one came wobbling along the row towards him, and he came up from underneath the way Annie had said, and there it was.

"Got it! Annie, I got it!"

"I know," said Annie. "I saw."

After that they got better and better. The tricky part was not the catching but what came next, because each butterfly had to come out of the net and into the tin without being hurt, and that meant slow hands and holding your breath, since a butterfly's wings are made of almost nothing at all.

They stayed out the whole afternoon. Big ones and small ones, plain brown ones and one or two worth stopping to look at properly. By the time the light began to go, there were somewhere between twenty and thirty butterflies in the tin, opening and closing their wings against the sides of it.

Brian sat back on the grass with the tin on his knees and looked at them.

"What do we feed them?" he said.

Annie did not answer for a moment.

"Nothing," she said. "We don't have anything."

Brian thought about that. There was nothing to feed them and nothing to keep them in, and no reason to keep them, and they had been perfectly all right where they were.

So he carried the tin out to the middle of the grass, and put both hands on the lid, and opened it.

They all went at once.

Not one at a time, not slowly. The whole afternoon's worth of butterflies came up out of that tin together in a single burst, twenty or thirty of them opening out in every direction over the grass and the starfruit trees, like a magic box being opened, like something a magician does before he holds up his empty hands.

Brian stood in the middle of the grass with the empty tin, and Annie stood beside him with her net down, and the two of them watched them go.

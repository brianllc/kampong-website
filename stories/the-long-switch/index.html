Lower Primary · ~1989 to 1991

The Long Switch

When Brian was a small boy, some of the best days were the ones he spent on buses with Dad.

They would walk down to the bus stop and climb aboard one of the old buses, the kind with no air-conditioning and windows you pushed up by hand, and ride all the way to Sim Lim Square. Dad was always building or fixing something at home, a radio, a clock, some gadget with its insides spilled out across the table, and Sim Lim was where the parts lived. Afterwards they would wander through the Sungei Road flea market nearby, where old men sat behind mats spread with watches and screwdrivers and televisions that might or might not still work.

"Where are we going today, Dad?" Brian asked, settling into the seat.

"Sim Lim," said Dad. "I need a few parts. And then we'll go for curry puffs afterwards."

That was all Brian needed to hear.

The bus rattled along, and Brian knelt up on the seat and watched the city slide past the window. There was so much of it.

"Dad, why do buses have so many windows?"

"So you can see out," said Dad. Then he reached up and pressed the long metal switch that ran the whole length of the ceiling, and a bell went ding somewhere near the front. "And so you know when to stop."

Sim Lim Square was a tower of shops stacked with all sorts of electronic parts, bright with white light and loud with a hundred gadgets playing at once. Dad moved through it as though he knew every corner, picking through bins of tiny parts that all looked the same to Brian.

"Dad, what's this?" Brian asked, picking a tiny black speck with three little metal legs out of one of the bins.

"Transistor," said Dad.

"What does it do?"

Dad took it from him and turned it slowly between his fingers. "It's a switch. A very small switch. Electricity comes, and this fellow decides. Let it pass, or stop it. On, off, on, off, faster than you can blink."

"Just on and off?"

"With just one of them, it can't do much," Dad agreed. He nodded at the bin, where there were thousands more exactly the same. "But put many of them together, and they can count. They can remember. They can even make a radio sing. There are thousands of them inside your television too, more than you could ever count."

Brian looked at the little black speck and tried to imagine thousands of them blinking on and off, fast enough to put a man inside the television.

"Something so small can do all that?" he said.

"Small," said Dad. "But never to be underestimated."

Dad had not forgotten the curry puffs either. When the shopping was done, they stopped at the little curry puff shop near the Rex cinema, the one with the queue out the front. The curry puffs came in a brown paper bag already going dark with oil, and they were so hot that Brian had to toss the first bite around his mouth, huffing, before he dared to chew. Inside was curried potato and a wedge of egg.

"Best curry puff in Singapore," Dad said, the way he said it every single time. "Don't tell your mother how many we ate."

On the bus home, full of curry puff and warm from the sun, Brian began to feel the floor swaying under him in a way he did not like at all.

"Dad," he said. "I don't feel so good."

Dad pushed the window up higher without a word, so the wind came in cool against Brian's face, and rested a hand on the back of his neck until the sick feeling passed.

After a while Brian looked up at the long switch running along the ceiling.

"Can I press it? To make the bus stop?"

Dad looked at the switch, then down at Brian, and his mouth twitched. "You're still too short for that. But one day you'll be tall enough."

Then he stood, slipped his hands under Brian's arms, and lifted him up towards the ceiling. "Go on," he said.

Brian stretched out a finger and pressed the long switch. The bell rang ding somewhere near the front, and the bus began to slow. Up there in Dad's arms, close to the warm ceiling, Brian felt as though he had done something enormous, and he promised himself that one day he would be tall enough to reach it on his own.

He did grow tall enough, in the end. But it took a few more years, and by then the buses had quietly changed. The windows were sealed shut now, and cool air hummed out of vents instead. The long switch that ran the length of the ceiling was gone. In its place were small red buttons set into the poles, low enough for any child to press.

By the time he could reach that ceiling on his own, there was nothing up there to reach for. Sometimes he would still lift his hand towards it to pretend he was pressing the long switch. ding The one press he had been saving all those years, the press he would finally do by himself, had quietly slipped away before he was ever big enough for it.

Kindergarten · ~1987

Spoons and Balloons

One evening, not long after he started kindergarten, Brian sat down at the table to do his very first piece of homework.

Ms Liew had given the class an assignment, and Brian was sure he had heard it correctly. They were to draw seven spoons.

He thought it would be easy. He had seen spoons all his life and held one at almost every meal. How hard could it be to draw seven of them?

Very hard, as it turned out.

The trouble was the curves. A spoon looked simple until you tried to draw one, and then it became a lopsided thing that refused to look like a spoon at all. Brian drew one. Its neck was too thin and looked like it would break at any moment. He rubbed it out and drew another. It looked too fat. He rubbed that one out too.

He finally managed to draw three decent spoons but no two of them looked the same. Brian's eyes were hot and blurry, and before he could stop himself, he was crying.

Dad heard him from the next room and came in.

"What's the matter?"

"I can't do it," Brian cried. "Ms Liew said draw seven spoons, and I just can't seem to get it."

Dad sat down beside him, picked up the pencil, and studied the page for a moment, the way he studied his broken radios.

"All right," he said. "Let's look at it properly. A spoon is just two shapes. An oval, here, for the bowl. And two lines at a slight angle, here, for the handle. You see? You don't draw a spoon. You draw an oval and two angled lines, and the spoon appears."

It was true. When Brian stopped trying to draw a whole spoon at once and drew it Dad's way instead, one piece at a time, the spoons came out clean and round and right. They worked through them together until there were seven good spoons standing in a neat row.

The next morning, Dad walked Brian to kindergarten. When they reached the classroom he went straight up to Ms Liew.

"Good morning," said Dad. "I have to ask. Seven spoons? That is a very unusual thing to set for five-year-olds. We were up half the night."

Ms Liew looked at him. Then she looked at the paper Brian was holding out, with its seven careful spoons.

"Spoons?" she said. "I asked the class to draw seven balloons."

Brian's face went hot all the way to his ears. Balloons. Of course. Round, easy balloons, that any child could draw, that did not have a single difficult curve in them.

Dad looked down at Brian, and his mouth twitched, and then he laughed, not unkindly at all.

"Never mind," he said. "It happens to the best of us."

And it was true that something had gone a little wrong. But it was also true that, thanks to Dad, Brian had brought to school that morning the seven finest spoons in the whole of kindergarten. They were the only seven spoons in a room full of balloons, and they were, without any doubt, the best.

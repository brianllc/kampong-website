Lower Primary · ~1990

Ah Ma

Brian's grandmother lived in the same flat as he did, and he never had a conversation with her in his life.

It was not anybody's fault. Ah Ma spoke Teochew. Brian and Annie spoke English, and a bit of Mandarin from school that was no use here at all, and between them and their grandmother there were perhaps five words that everybody understood. So there was a greeting in the morning and a greeting when he came in from playing, and that was the whole of it.

"Ah Ma," he would say.

She would say something back, short, in her own language, and nod at him, and that was that. He had no idea what she thought about anything.

She had her own days. Most mornings she went down to the void deck, or along to the kopitiam, where there were other old ladies who spoke what she spoke, and she would sit there for hours. Sometimes when Brian came pounding down the stairs on his way to the playground he would go past her sitting with her friends, and one of them would say something and they would all look at him and laugh, and he would keep running.

That was his grandmother. An old lady in his flat with her own friends and her own language and her own life, going on quietly alongside his.

Then she started to get ill.

Brian was not told very much about it, which is usually how it goes. There were more doctors. There were conversations that stopped when he came into the room. Then one day Dad explained that Ah Ma was going to a home where there were nurses, because she needed looking after properly now, and there was nobody in the flat during the day to do it.

The home was in Bukit Timah, near Beauty World, a long way across the island from Teck Whye.

So the weekends changed shape. Every weekend the whole family got in and went across to visit her, and they would sit in a room that smelt of disinfectant with other people's grandmothers in the other beds, and Dad would talk to her in Teochew, and Mum would talk to her in Teochew, and Brian would say "Ah Ma" and then stand there.

Then one morning there was a phone call, and Ah Ma had died in the night, in her sleep, without any fuss at all.

Dad arranged the funeral. In those days a funeral was held at the void deck of your own block, that being what void decks were for, but the void deck of Block 15 had already been booked by somebody else for that week, so it had to be Block 17 instead, the next block along.

That was where Brian went to his first funeral, at the age of eight, one block from home. He had never been to one before. Both his grandfathers had died before he was born, so nobody had ever had to explain any of this to him, and now it was all being explained at once.

They were given white to wear, plain and rough, and a small square of blue cloth was pinned to the sleeve, which meant that you were family. Everybody in blue was hers.

The funeral ran three days.

On the first day the relatives came, and the neighbours came, and people Brian had never seen in his life came and looked at him and told him how big he was. There were tables. There were incense sticks and a photograph of Ah Ma looking younger and more serious than he remembered her. There was a great deal of standing about.

And it rained.

It rained the way it had never rained in Brian's life, which was a short life, but even so. It came down all day without stopping, so loud on the roof of the void deck that people had to lean in to hear each other. The drains filled and then overflowed. The grass went under. Out past the shelter the whole world was grey and roaring and you could not see the next block.

"Wah," said one of his aunties, standing at the edge of it with her arms folded. "So heavy. Even heaven is crying for her."

The others agreed that this was so. Somebody said that the sky knew, and somebody else said that rain like this on the first day was a good sign, although Brian did not catch what it was a good sign of.

Then Brian noticed the worms.

The ground had taken all the water it could and the earthworms were coming up out of it. They were coming up everywhere, all across the soaked grass, hundreds of them, more worms than he had ever imagined were down there in the first place. And the water running off the grass was carrying them into the longkang, so that the drain was not running with water any more. It was running with worms, turning over and over and sliding away past the funeral in their hundreds.

Brian stood at the edge of the void deck and watched them go for a long time.

Nobody told him not to. There is not much for an eight-year-old to do at a funeral, and the adults had their own business, and so he spent a good part of the first day of his grandmother's funeral looking into a drain.

By the second day the rain had stopped.

The sun came out and the hill did what hills do, and the water ran off down Teck Whye Lane and away, and by the afternoon the ground was dry again and you would not have known.

That was the night they burnt the house.

It had been standing at the side all along and Brian had been looking at it since the first morning. It was a palace, made of paper and bamboo, taller than Dad, with a roof and windows and a door that opened. There were paper cars. There were paper servants standing in a row, with paper faces. All of it was for Ah Ma, to have over there.

Brian did not know whether he believed any of it. He was eight. Nobody asked his opinion, and he did not have a settled one, and what he did have was the plain fact that they were about to set fire to a paper palace in a car park, which was the most extraordinary thing that had ever been proposed in his hearing.

They carried it out and lit it, and it went up enormously.

The flames went far higher than the palace itself, and the heat came off it in a wall so that everybody stepped back, and pieces of burnt paper lifted off and went up into the dark and drifted away over the block. The paper servants went. The cars went. The roof fell in and the whole thing collapsed into a heap of gold and orange with the bamboo frame glowing inside it.

Then his auntie put a bamboo stick into his hand.

"Beat the ground," she said. "Like this."

"Why?"

"So the house reaches her. She must hear it. If she hears it she will know it came from you, and she will look after you."

So Brian beat the ground.

He beat it with everything he had, along with all his cousins and his aunties and his uncles, the whole family in white with their blue cloths, hammering the concrete around a burning house while the smoke went up over Block 17. It made a tremendous noise. His arms ached the next day.

He did not know whether it worked. He knew that he had done it properly.

The fire went down eventually into a black patch and some ash, and it was late by then, and they walked back to Block 15 and went to bed.

On the third day they took Ah Ma to Mandai to be cremated, and afterwards her ashes were placed in a temple in Bukit Timah, not far from where she had spent her last months.

And that evening, at home, Dad sat down and did not do anything for a while.

"Both of them are gone now," he said, to nobody in particular. "My mother and my father."

Brian did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing.

"All my life there has been a row of people in front of me," Dad said. "My parents, my uncles, all of that generation. And a row behind me, which is you and your sisters. I was in the middle, and I never had to think about it."

He turned his cup round on the table.

"Now the row in front is gone. There is nobody left in it. It feels strange. As though everybody has moved up and I have been left standing at the front."

Brian was eight. He understood about half of that, and the half he understood was that his father was sad. The rest of it took a very long time to arrive, and it did arrive, in the end.

"But that is how it goes," said Dad. "Everybody gets their turn in the middle. It was their turn to look after me. Now it is my turn to look after you, and after your children when they come."

Then he got up and went to see about dinner.

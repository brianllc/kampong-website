Upper Primary · 1992

The Missing Line

Of all the things Brian dreaded, the worst was the weekly 听写.

Every week, Zhuo Laoshi read out a long list of characters, and the class wrote them down in their squarish exercise books. And every week she marked them by a rule that never changed. Five marks off for each character you got wrong. Fifty was the passing mark. And for every five marks you fell below fifty, you held out your hand for one stroke of the cane.

Brian usually scored somewhere between thirty and fifty, which meant that most weeks, he was caned.

He knew the routine so well that it had almost stopped frightening him. When the marked books came back, the boys and girls who had failed would shuffle to the front and line up. Zhuo Laoshi kept a thin rattan cane on her desk. She was not cruel about it. She did not scold or lecture. She simply read out your score, waited for you to put out your hand, and brought the cane down, once for every five marks, quick and stinging. Then you went back to your seat and shook your hand under the desk until the burning faded.

Then, one week, something happened that had never happened before.

Brian got his book back, opened it, and saw a number at the top he had to read twice. Fifty-five.

Fifty-five. Above the line. For the first time he could remember, he was not going to be caned.

"Eh! Fifty-five!" he whispered to Shunlai, holding up the book. He felt as though he might float right out of his chair.

But Brian had failed so many 听写 that he had learned to check them carefully, hunting through the page for any half-mark that might pull him up or drag him down. And as he went back over it, his stomach slowly turned to stone.

That week there had been a 默写 as well, a passage they had to write out from memory. And there, in the middle of his, was a gap. Brian had skipped a whole line. He had not written it at all. Zhuo Laoshi had marked the line above it and the line below it, found them both correct, and never noticed that the one in between was missing.

Those lost characters should have pulled his fifty-five down well past the fifty he needed. He had not passed at all. Somewhere inside that fifty-five was a caning he had truly earned.

Nobody knew. Nobody would ever know. The book was marked. The fifty-five sat there in red ink at the top of the page, in Zhuo Laoshi's own hand. All Brian had to do was close the book and say nothing, and for the first time in months he would walk home without a stinging palm.

He sat very still, looking at the gap where the line should have been.

Then he closed the book and, before he could change his mind, he walked up to the front of the class.

"Laoshi," he said. His voice came out smaller than he had meant it to. "I think you marked mine wrongly. I left out a whole line here. In the 默写."

Zhuo Laoshi took the book. She found the gap, and counted along the missing line with the tip of her pen, her face giving nothing away. Then she drew a line through the fifty-five and wrote a new number beneath it. Thirty-five.

"Hold out your hand," she said.

So Brian held out his hand and took the caning he had so very nearly escaped. It hurt exactly as much as it always did.

Back in his seat, shaking his hot palm under the desk, he found Shunlai staring at him as though he had grown a second head.

"You siao ah?" Shunlai whispered. "Already fifty-five, leh. Why you go and tell?"

Brian opened his mouth, and found that he had no answer that would make any sense to Shunlai. He could not really explain it, even to himself.

He only knew that the fifty-five had sat heavier in his chest than any caning ever had. And now, with his palm still burning and a proper failing mark in his book, that heaviness was gone.

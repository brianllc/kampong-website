Lower Primary · June 1990

The New Compound

The school moved during the June holidays, which was clever of it, because it meant that no child had to do anything at all. Brian went off for the holidays from one school and came back from them to another one.

The new compound was at Cashew Road, and Cashew Road was too far to walk.

That was the first thing that changed. For as long as Brian had been going to school he had gone there on his legs, and now there was a school bus, and the school bus came to the car park.

It was painted beige and it was always a little early, and Mum would send them off from the car park.

"Have a great day at school," she would say, as they got on.

The bus went out of Teck Whye and stopped at Block 20 and picked up more of them, and then went on to Phoenix Park and picked up more still, and by the time it turned in at Cashew Road it was full of children with their bags on their laps, and the noise inside it was tremendous.

Then everybody got off, and stopped talking, and looked.

The old school had been made of wood. Brian had never particularly thought about that until the moment he stood in front of a school that was made of concrete, and was several storeys high, and went on and on, and had not a single mark on any part of it. It was enormous. It was the newest thing he had ever seen. Nobody had leaned on it yet.

"Wah," said Wei Hao, from somewhere behind him. "So big."

Mrs Koh took them round it that first morning, the whole class in a line behind her, and it was less a tour than a series of announcements.

Here was the classroom. The tables had plastic tops, smooth and hard, on black metal legs, with a metal shelf underneath for your books so that nothing had to sit on the floor any more. Brian put both hands flat on his and slid them about on the plastic.

And the room was bright. That was the thing he noticed and kept noticing all morning. They were up on a higher floor, and one whole side of the classroom was glass louvres, and the sun came through them in long slats and lay across the tables and the floor in stripes. The old classrooms had been dim, in the way that low wooden rooms under trees are dim. This one was full of light.

Above them, two great ventilation fans turned slowly.

"You cannot switch those on yourselves," said Mrs Koh.

Then the library, which was air-conditioned, and the music room, which was air-conditioned as well, so that opening the door was like putting your face in a fridge. Some of them wanted to stand in the doorway and were moved along.

Then downstairs to the canteen. The wooden benches from the old school, the ones with the split in them that ate the hem of your shorts, were gone. In their place were long new tables with benches attached, and along one whole side ran a row of canteen stalls, more stalls than the old school had had. Next to the canteen was a shop that sold pens and pencils and notebooks and erasers and rulers, which meant that a boy with money in his pocket could simply walk down and buy a pencil, at school, whenever he liked.

And there were two water coolers.

"You press the button," said Mrs Koh, and pressed it, and the water came out in a small arc, and she stood aside.

The first boy drank and came away with his eyes wide. "Eh. It's cold."

It was not cold like a tap. It was cold like a drink out of the fridge at home, at ten in the morning, in the middle of a school. They queued up all over again to have another go, and Mrs Koh let them, and then moved them on outdoors.

Outdoors there was a basketball court. There was a hard court beside it. And beyond both of them there was a soccer field, wide and open and green and completely empty, with the grass still short and new, and nothing at all growing along the edge of it. Brian stood and looked at that for a while.

Then somebody at the front of the line shouted, and the line came apart.

There was a millipede on the wall.

It was as long as a finger, dark and glossy, going up the concrete on its unhurried hundred legs and not the least bit worried about any of them. And once they had seen one they saw the others, because the others were everywhere. On the staircase walls. Along the bottom of the corridor, where the floor met the wall. At the edges of the flower beds in dozens, coming in off the grass.

Half the class went towards them and half the class went backwards.

"Aiyah, it's only millipede," said Wei Hao, picking one off the wall.

"Don't bring it here," said a girl, several steps away, with her hands up. "Don't. Don't."

"They are harmless," said Mrs Koh, who had seen this coming. "Millipedes are not centipedes. Centipedes bite. Millipedes eat leaves and they mind their own business."

"Teacher, why got so many?"

"Because everything here is new," she said. "All the soil for the flowers and the shrubs was brought in when they built the school. There must have been eggs in it. And then it was the June holidays and there was nobody here at all, so they hatched, and they had the whole place to themselves for a month."

Brian thought that was a very good arrangement for the millipedes and said so, to nobody in particular.

For the boys it settled the matter entirely. They were boys who had spent their lives catching things: spiders out of bushes, fighting fish, beetles, whatever came. A school that produced millipedes on its own walls, free, without anybody having to look for them, was not a problem with the school. It was a feature of it. The girls kept their distance for months and never really came round.

Brian picked one off the staircase wall on the way back up to the bright classroom, and it curled itself immediately into a tight flat coil in his palm, the way they do, and stopped being a hundred legs and became a small dark spring instead.

He carried it up and set it on the plastic top of his brand new table, in a slat of sun from the louvres, and stood there with the whole shining school going on around him, waiting for it to decide that he was safe and come out again.

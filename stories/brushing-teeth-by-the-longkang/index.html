Lower Primary · ~1990

Brushing Teeth by the Longkang

At Bukit Panjang Primary, everybody's teeth were the school's business.

Whatever else the school cared about, it cared about teeth. There were posters. There were talks. There was a chart. And in the list of things you had to bring to school, along with your books and your pencil case, were a toothbrush, a tube of toothpaste and a mug.

Every day after recess, the whole school brushed its teeth.

There were far too many children for the sinks. Thirty or forty boys and girls to a class and only a handful of taps, so what happened instead was that everybody filled their mugs, went outside, and squatted down along the longkang.

The longkang was the open drain that ran along the edge of the corridor, and it was, on the whole, an excellent arrangement. You went down on your heels in a long row with your mug beside you and your elbows on your knees, and you brushed, and when you were done you spat into the drain and rinsed and spat again, and a thin white river of everybody's toothpaste went sliding away down the concrete.

Brian liked it. It was the only part of the school day where the entire class was on the floor, in a line, all doing exactly the same thing, and nobody had to be quiet.

"Look," said Wei Hao, one day, with his mouth full of foam.

Brian looked. Wei Hao had made a beard.

Then one afternoon Mrs Koh came out with a small box.

"After you brush," she said, "come and take one of these. Chew it. Do not swallow it."

Inside the box were little tablets, and they were blue.

The whole class brushed with unusual enthusiasm that day, because something was clearly about to happen and nobody wanted to be caught out by it. Then they queued up, and took a tablet each, and chewed.

"Now show your teeth to the person next to you."

Brian turned to Wei Hao and pulled his lips back.

Wei Hao started laughing so hard that he had to hold on to the wall.

Every one of them was blue. Not all over. That was the horrible part. It came in patches, along the gums, in the corners, in the gaps, thick blue smears on some teeth and none at all on others, so that every mouth in that corridor looked like a badly painted fence.

"Wah, yours is damn blue," said Wei Hao.

"Yours also."

"Not so blue as yours."

They were all delighted, and then Mrs Koh explained what the blue meant, and they stopped being delighted.

The tablet, she said, stuck to the places you had not cleaned. So the blue was not a game. The blue was the map of everywhere their brushing had missed, printed on their own teeth for everybody to see.

"Now go back and brush again," said Mrs Koh, "until it is gone."

So back they all went to the longkang, and squatted down again, and this time nobody talked.

Brian brushed the way he had never brushed in his life. He got into the corners. He did the backs, which he had privately considered optional. He went along the gum line where the worst of it was, again and again, and checked, and there was still blue, and went at it again.

Some of it came away easily. Some of it took a very long time. And there was a patch at the back of his bottom teeth that he had clearly not touched with a brush in months, which stayed obstinately blue long after everybody else had finished and gone in.

When he finally straightened up his legs had gone dead from squatting.

Down in the longkang, the water ran away past him, and it was not white that afternoon. It was blue, all the way along the row, blue from thirty sets of now clean teeth, sliding off down the drain and out of the school and away.

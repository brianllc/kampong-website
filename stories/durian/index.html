Durian

<!-- status: awaiting read · level: P3 · year: 1991 -->

The smell came into the flat before Dad did.

Brian was on the living room floor with a comic when it reached him, sweet and thick and a little rotten all at once, and so strong that it seemed to have weight to it. He sat up.

"Brian! Come and hold the door," Dad called from the landing. "I've got durians."

He was outside with a gunny sack held against his chest in both arms, and the sack had corners coming out of it in every direction. Brian pressed himself flat against the door while Dad shuffled sideways through it, and the smell came through with him and filled the whole flat in about four seconds.

"In the kitchen," Mum said, already coming out of it. "Not on my table. On the floor."

She spread out yesterday's newspaper over the kitchen tiles, several sheets thick, and Dad tipped the sack over so that the durians rolled out onto it, brown and spiked and heavier than they looked. Annie was sitting down before the last one had stopped moving. Mum sat. Dad sat. Brian sat too, a little further back than the rest of them, because Brian had never in his life eaten a durian.

It was the smell. Every year the season came and every year the family sat on the kitchen floor like this, and every year Brian sat with them and ate nothing, which was fine by him. He liked the sitting on the floor part. He liked that his mother and father and sister were all in one place and all cross-legged and all in a good mood, which did not happen at the dinner table. He just did not see why any of it had to involve that smell.

Dad took out his jack knife and opened it. It was a proper metal one that lived in his pocket, and he turned the first durian over in his hands the way he turned everything over in his hands before he started.

"Look at this," he said. "You see these lines?"

Brian leaned in. Under the spikes, running from the stem down to the base, there were faint seams in the shell, like the segments of an orange drawn from the outside.

"Every durian tells you where to open it," Dad said. "You don't cut anywhere you like. You find the line, you put the knife in the line, and then you let the durian do the rest."

He set the point in and gave it a twist, and the whole thing split along the seam with a dry crack, and then his thumbs went into the gap and it opened out into two halves like a book. Inside, in the pale hollows of the shell, sat the flesh, yellow and soft and folded over itself.

Annie had a piece out and in her mouth before Dad had finished opening the second one.

There was a worm in the third. Brian saw it first, small and pale, curled up in a hollow near the seed.

"There's a worm," he said.

Annie screamed and moved backwards about a foot.

Dad looked at it for a moment. "Good," he said joyfully. "That means they're fresh. The worm knows how to choose better than the uncle at the stall."

"Try one," Mum said to Brian. "One bite now that you are older."

"One bite," said Annie, with her mouth full.

So Brian took a piece, and held his breath, and put it in his mouth.

It was nothing like the smell. It was sweet, and thick, and it went soft against the roof of his mouth like custard, and underneath the sweetness there was something almost bitter that made him want another one straight away to work out what it was. He swallowed and looked at his fingers as though they had tricked him.

"Well?" said Dad.

Brian did not answer, because he was already reaching into the shell.

He finished that durian by himself. Somewhere in the middle of it, without his noticing when, the smell in the kitchen stopped being a bad smell. It was the same smell. It was just that it now did not seem so bad, and maybe even bordering on pleasant.

When they had finished, Mum took one of the empty husks and stood it up on the newspaper like a bowl. She poured water into the hollow where the flesh had been, tipped in a little salt from her hand, and swirled it round with her fingers.

"Drink," she said. "Both of you. Otherwise you'll be heaty tomorrow."

Annie drank first and passed it over. It was faintly salty, and faintly of durian, and slightly gritty at the bottom, and Brian sat on the newspaper on his mother's kitchen floor and drank it down to the end.

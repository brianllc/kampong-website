The Pavilion

<!-- status: awaiting read · level: P5 · year: 1993 -->

Everybody in Singapore was watching the football that year.

The team was doing well up in the Malaysian league, better than anybody could remember, and for one whole year football was simply what there was. It was on the television and it was in the newspaper and it was in the corridors at school. Boys who had never kicked anything in their lives suddenly had opinions about formations. Brian had opinions too, and no idea where he had got them from.

Then the holidays came, and the whole arrangement had to change.

The dodgeball had only ever worked because of the bus. Five boys going home on the same 175 meant five boys standing in the same void deck every afternoon, and no school meant no bus, and no bus meant no game. For about a day this seemed like a serious problem.

Then Derrick pointed out that he had a pavilion.

It stood between two blocks near his flat in Choa Chu Kang, a long sheltered space with a roof over it and a walkway running down either side. It belonged to everybody and to nobody. When there was a Malay wedding it filled with chairs and lights and people in beautiful clothes. When there was a Chinese funeral it filled with a tent and white cloth and a great many relatives. And on every other day of the year, which was most days, it was simply an enormous empty space with a roof on, standing there with nothing whatever to do.

For five boys of ten and eleven, it was an indoor soccer court. The pillars along the two walkways even made the goalposts.

So that was the holidays.

Brian would have his lunch at home, and then get up from the table and say he was going out.

"Where?"

"Derrick's."

"Be back before dark," Mum would say, which was the rule, and which he mostly kept.

Then he took the 307 out to Choa Chu Kang, got off, and walked along a small footpath. And there they would meet, in the pavilion.

They had the five-boys problem again, of course. Five will not make two teams any more in football than it will in dodgeball, and they had solved it once already, so they solved it again. Three against two. If the three won, the two got to choose one of them to come over, which made it three against two the other way round, and so the sides kept changing all afternoon.

The pavilion made everything better. The roof meant the sun could not stop them and the rain could not either. The low walls along the sides usually kept the ball in while letting the wind through. First to five goals won, and then the sides changed and it started again.

They played for hours. Whole afternoons went into that pavilion and came out again as nothing at all. There had been game after game and somebody had won every one of them, and by the time they stopped not one of them could have told you who.

And then, at the end of it, there were three ways the day could go.

Sometimes it simply ran out, and they picked up their things and went home, and Brian got the bus back to Teck Whye with Weijia and Shunlai.

Sometimes they went up to Derrick's flat instead, where the comics were, and lay about on the floor of his room reading, five sweaty boys in a small bedroom, saying almost nothing for an hour.

And sometimes, best of all, somebody would say the words seven eleven, and all five of them would walk round to the nearest 7-11.

They came out with the biggest Slurpee their pooled money would buy, and took turns drinking from it, not saying much, in the last of the afternoon.

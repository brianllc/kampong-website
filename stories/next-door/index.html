Lower Primary · ~1990

Next Door

There was only one other flat on the landing, and in it lived three sisters.

Their names were Xiuping, Xiulan and Xiujuan, which Brian thought was an excellent system, and not unlike his own, since he and Annie and Cherie went A, B, C. The three sisters were teenagers. That meant they were tall, and had opinions, and were allowed out, and knew things.

It also meant they had clothes.

This was the important part. Three teenage girls in one flat produce a truly astonishing quantity of clothes, and one afternoon somebody had the idea of putting some of them on Brian and Annie.

"Come here," said Xiulan. "Stand still."

Brian stood still. Things went over his head and were pulled down and tugged about and rejected and replaced. A belt went round him twice. Somebody put a hat on him.

"Now walk."

"Walk where?"

"To the end of the room and back. Like a model. Slowly."

So Brian walked to the end of the room and back, in a hat and a belt that went round him twice, and the three of them watched him from the bed and clapped, and Annie came out next in something with shoulders in it, and after that it became a regular event.

They took it seriously, which was why it was fun. There were rounds. There were themes. Xiujuan did the announcing and Xiuping did the wardrobe, and Annie, who had her own ideas about what suited her, argued about every single item.

"I am not wearing that."

"It is the best one."

"It is green."

"You look nice in green."

"I look like a vegetable."

Brian wore whatever he was given. He did not much care what he had on. What he liked was the walking to the end of the room, and the clapping, and being, for about forty minutes at a stretch, the most interesting thing in the flat.

But the fashion show was not the best thing next door.

The best thing next door was the room itself.

Because the three of them read. All of them, all the time, the way some families are about football, and their bedroom had books along one wall and books stacked on the floor beside the bed and books lying open face down on things, and to Brian, coming in from a flat with a shelf and a half in it, that room was not a bedroom at all. It was a library.

"Can I look?"

"You can look," said Xiuping. "Don't fold the pages."

So he looked. He went along the shelf with his head on one side reading the spines, the way you do, and every so often he pulled one out and opened it in the middle to see what it was made of.

And then, after a while, they started lending them to him.

That was how Brian read Around the World in Eighty Days, sitting on the floor of somebody else's bedroom and then on the floor of his own, entirely convinced that Phileas Fogg was not going to make it. That was how he read Little Women, which he would not have chosen for himself in a hundred years, and which he finished anyway and then thought about for days afterwards. Books came across that landing one at a time, and went back, and were exchanged for others.

And one of them was a book about a Japanese girl called Totto-chan.

She got into trouble for things that were not really trouble. She asked questions nobody wanted asked. And on her very first day at her old school she was sent to stand by the window, and she leaned out of it and talked to the street musicians going past, and it went on like that until the school could not stand her any more.

Brian read it on the floor of the room he shared with Annie, with the radio going somewhere behind him, and he had never in his life read a book that seemed to be about somebody he actually knew. She was about his age. She was at school, the way he was at school, and she was getting into the kind of trouble that did not seem to him like trouble at all.

Then he took it back across the landing, because it was not his.

And then he borrowed it again.

Of everything on that shelf, Totto-chan was the one he kept going back for. He read it more times than he could have counted, and it never once wore out, and every time he handed it back he already knew he would be over for it again.

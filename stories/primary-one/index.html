Lower Primary · 1989

Primary One

On the first day of Primary One, Brian spent the whole morning waiting.

In those days, primary schools in Singapore ran two sessions. Primary Two, Four and Six went to school in the morning, and Primary One, Three and Five went in the afternoon. So new pupils like Brian did not set off until after lunch, which meant a whole long morning at home first, too excited to play, watching the hours crawl by. At last he ate an early lunch he was too nervous to finish, and put on a crisp new uniform that still had the fold lines pressed into it.

Mum and Dad took him and his sister to school by bus. Brian's school was Bukit Panjang Primary, the same school his elder sister Annie went to, which made it feel a little less frightening.

They walked in through the main gate in a slow river of parents and children, past the morning pupils streaming the other way towards home, and Brian looked about him with wide eyes. To the right was an enormous field, far bigger than any field he had ever played on. To the left stood the school building, two whole storeys of it, long and low beneath a sloping roof of faded red tiles. Rows of wooden-framed windows had been propped open along its length, and an open corridor ran past the classrooms on each floor, so that the breeze could blow straight through. There was a small patch thick with plants, a little garden and farm, and Brian was almost sure he saw a papaya tree growing right there in the school grounds. The road curved in and ended at a small roundabout, and in the middle of the roundabout grew a bright clump of red hibiscus.

A teacher led the new pupils to their classroom and told the parents, kindly but firmly, that it was time to go. Brian watched Mum and Dad join the crowd of grown-ups drifting back towards the gate. Then he turned around to face the room, and his new life, on his own.

The first thing the teacher did was line everybody up outside the classroom, shortest to tallest.

"We will sit by height," she said, "so that everyone can see the board."

Brian had been born in October, near the very end of the year, which made him one of the youngest in the class, and one of the shortest too. He shuffled into his place near the front of the line, and when they filed in, he was given a seat in the second row.

It was a big wooden room with a high, sloping ceiling, and one whole wall was made of windows, row upon row of them, tilted open to let in the afternoon light. The desks were old and wooden, set close together in straight lines, and along one wall a soft board was pinned with charts and bright pictures and a calendar. Brian sat very still in his place and looked at it all.

When the recess bell rang, the class was marched to the canteen. It was a great open-air hall under a high roof of corrugated zinc, with long wooden tables and benches set out in rows across a bare cement floor. Stalls lined one side, steaming and clattering, and a red soft-drink sign glowed at the far end, and the whole place rang with noise and the smell of cooking. Brian stood at the edge of it, not at all sure what to do, until a familiar voice called his name.

It was Annie, his older sister.

"Over here," she said, waving him across. She was two years older and knew exactly how everything worked. She helped him buy a plate of dark, glistening noodles, the kind Brian always called black noodles, and pressed it into his hands. "Eat quickly," she told him. "Recess is short."

So Brian ate quickly, faster than he ever ate at home, and the black noodles were the best thing he had ever tasted, perhaps because he had earned them, on his first day, all by himself.

There was still a little time left when he finished, so Brian wandered out to the big field to look at it properly. He was standing at the edge of it, alone, when a boy from his class came running up, pink-faced and out of breath.

"Eh," said the boy. "We're playing catching. Want to join?"

Brian looked across the field. A pack of children from his row and the rows behind were already tearing across the grass after one another, shrieking.

"Okay!" said Brian.

"I'm Wei Hao," said the boy, already turning to run. "Come on. You're 'it'!"

And then he was off, and Brian was after him, running out across the great field in the afternoon sun, and the field did not feel so enormous any more.

Upper Primary

Pang Sua Pond

<!-- status: awaiting read · level: P5 · year: 1993 -->

Shunlai told them about it at recess.

"I found a place," he said. "Behind the pond."

Everybody knew the pond. It sat beside the main road in Bukit Panjang and the bus went past it twice a day, once going to school and once coming home, and there was nothing whatever to look at. It had been under construction for as long as any of them could remember. What it amounted to was a great sloping wall of grey rocks, each one bigger than a boy, stacked and fitted together all the way down, with water sitting at the bottom of it. Everybody understood that it was there to collect the rain. Nobody had ever had a single thought about it beyond that.

"Behind it," said Shunlai. "Not the front. You can get down."

"Down where?"

"Down to the water."

That afternoon they did not play dodgeball and they did not go home. They got on the bus as usual, and then halfway along Shunlai stood up and rang the bell, and five boys got off at a stop none of them had ever used before.

There was a new bridge across, made with freshly painted wooden beams. They went over it and round to the back of the pond, where nobody went, because there was nothing behind a pond.

And Shunlai was right. On that side, the rocks were not a wall. They were steps.

Not proper steps. Enormous boulders, grey and rounded, sitting on top of one another all the way down on a slope, with ferns and long grasses coming up out of every gap between them, and gaps deep enough to lose a leg in.

The five of them stood at the top with their school bags on and looked down.

"How far is it?"

"Not so far."

"Looks far," said Weijia.

"If you slip," said Yehao, "you might break something."

"Then don't slip lah," said Shunlai.

Brian looked at the rocks and thought about what would happen if a person slipped. Then he looked at the other four, and none of them was leaving, and neither was he.

They went down one at a time, sitting on the edge of each rock and dropping to the next, gingerly, testing everything before they put weight on it. It was not graceful, but it was easier than it had looked from the top.

Then they were at the bottom, and it was another place altogether.

They had come about the height of a flat and a half, no more, and the whole world had changed. Trees leaned in over the top from both banks and closed over them, so that when Brian looked up there was no road and no blocks of flats, only leaves with the light coming through them in pieces. It was cooler down there. Ahead of them the ground opened out again and they could see the pond, and past it the bus stop they had come from, which they could look at now without anybody looking back.

At the bottom of it all ran a stream, hardly a stream at all, wide and shallow and the colour of milk tea, sliding over a bed of sand with rocks standing about in it. In one place it dropped a foot or so between two boulders and turned white and made a noise entirely out of proportion to its size. Ferns grew out of the rock face on the far bank in a thick green curtain. Nothing about any of it looked as though anybody had put it there, although of course somebody had.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Yehao found the rock.

It was a big one, flat on top and dry, sitting a little way out of the water and easily large enough for five, and they climbed onto it and took off their bags and sat down. Then Derrick opened his bag and brought out what he had bought at the canteen and had not eaten, and after that everybody did, and it turned out that between them they had a fairly serious amount of food.

So they had a picnic, on a rock, under the trees, at the bottom of a stormwater pond in Bukit Panjang.

It was a good afternoon for it. The heat had gone out of the day and the light came down green through the leaves, and they ate everything they had and sat on with their feet up on the warm stone, watching the little waterfall going over and over the same two rocks and not going anywhere at all.

Nobody up on the road knew they were there.

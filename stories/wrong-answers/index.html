Lower Primary

Wrong Answers

Nobody told them what the GEP test was for.

They were told there would be a test, and that it was not like the other tests, and that they should simply do their best. It had no subject. It was not Chinese or Mathematics or Science, and there was nothing whatever to revise for it, which ought to have been the best news Brian had received all year.

Then Mrs Koh said one more thing on her way out of the room.

"If you do very well," she said, "you may be moved to another school."

Brian's ears perked up.

He looked round the classroom. Weijia was beside him, drawing on his eraser. Wei Hao was two rows ahead with his chair tipped back. All his best friends were in this school, and a good number of them were inside this room, and somebody was proposing that the reward for doing well was to be moved to a different school.

He decided, before Mrs Koh had reached the door, that he was not going to be one of them.

So he settled on a number. He would score seventy-five, which would be average and decent and not outstanding.

It turned out to be harder than he thought, because the questions were not easy enough for him to be sure which of his answers were right in the first place.

The questions were very different from the ones they had done before. Some were shapes, shapes that turned into other shapes, and rows of things where one thing did not belong, and folded squares you had to picture unfolded. There almost seemed to be no fixed logic to them until you found it. And Brian found that he quite enjoyed identifying the patterns.

When he had finished he went back through the paper and sorted his answers into two piles, the ones he was certain of and the ones he was guessing at. Then he assumed half the guesses would go his way, worked out how far above seventy-five that left him, and spoiled exactly that many of the ones he was sure about.

In January he went up to Primary Four in the same school, with Weijia beside him, learning to do spins with his pen.

Author's note: GEP stands for the Gifted Education Programme, which Singapore started in 1984. Pupils were screened in Primary Three, and the small number who were chosen went into special classes, which in those years usually meant moving to one of the few schools that ran them.

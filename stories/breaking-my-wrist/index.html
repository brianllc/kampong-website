Upper Primary · 1992

Breaking My Wrist

In 1992, when Brian was in Primary Four, his HDB block was wrapped in scaffolding for repainting. The workers blasted the old paint off the walls with waterjets, and green netting was hung around the ground floor to catch the falling water and paint chips so they would not land on people walking past.

When the weather was nice and warm, Brian and his friend Yongjie would play badminton just below the block. One day Brian hit the shuttlecock too hard. It flew up high and caught at the top of the scaffolding, and there it stayed, a white spot on the green netting.

"Oh no, how now?" said Yongjie. "That was our only shuttlecock."

Brian looked up. The shuttlecock was right there at the edge of the scaffolding. It looked easy to reach.

He wrapped his legs around one of the poles and pulled himself up, bit by bit. Up close, the scaffolding smelt of paint and warm metal. With a swift flick of his right hand he caught the shuttlecock by its feathers, and at the same moment his left hand slipped on the smooth pole. For a half-second, Brian felt that time had stopped and he was just floating in the air. Then his left hand shot out to break his fall, and he heard a sound like a stick snapping inside him.

At first, Brian was so proud of how quickly he had caught his fall. Then his wrist began to throb.

"I think I need to get home," Brian told Yongjie. Yongjie could only nod as he was still in shock over the fall.

By the time he reached home, his wrist was swollen and a strange purplish blue.

"Tsk, what happened?" said Mum, taking his hand. "Come, let's bring you to the sinseh to see what's wrong. My sister, your aunt, knows of a good one at Ang Mo Kio."

The sinseh's clinic was a long bus ride away, on the sixth floor of an HDB block, for the sinseh saw his patients out of his own flat. It smelt of dried herbs and something sharp Brian could not name. Glass jars filled with bark and curled roots and unknown things lined the shelves, and an old black clock ticked above the doorway. The sinseh himself was a small old man in a singlet, with very kind eyes and a single grey hair sticking out of his chin.

He turned Brian's wrist this way and that, very gently. Then he looked up and asked, "Little boy, why do you smoke when you are so young?"

Brian blinked. "I don't smoke. I'm just a kid."

"Then why is there a cigarette packet behind you?"

Brian turned to look. And crack. A sharp pain shot up his arm so fast he barely had time to yelp before it was gone.

There was no cigarette packet. The sinseh had quietly set the bone while Brian wasn't looking. Brian was too astonished to react. Years later, he would still remember the small, satisfied smile on the old man's face, as if proud of his trick.

"I've straightened the bone," the sinseh said to Mum. "But it seems to be a fracture. He'll need to take an X-ray at the hospital to confirm."

The hospital X-ray that afternoon confirmed a hairline crack at the wrist. Brian came home with a heavy white plaster cast that ran from his knuckles all the way to his elbow, and with strict instructions from the doctor not to get it wet for three whole weeks.

That evening, Dad sat down beside him on the sofa. He looked at the cast for a long moment and gave Brian a serious look.

"Well, you were both reckless and brave today," Dad said. "Reckless for climbing the scaffolding and brave for dealing with the pain. If you stay brave for the next three weeks, I'll get you that Super Soaker you've been asking for. Deal?"

"Deal," said Brian, trying not to grin too widely.

The next morning, Brian went to school and hid his casted arm under the table. He had been worried about what everyone would say. Lucky for him, it was his left arm, so he could still write with his right hand.

But of all things, the lesson that day was about an injured man with a broken arm. Brian's deskmate Weijia pointed straight at him and shouted, "Just like Brian's!"

There was no hiding it after that, as his teacher asked about it and his classmates crowded around to look. By recess, his cast was covered in coloured marker. Get well soon! from one boy. A wobbly drawing of a smiling face from another. Yongjie wrote his name in giant letters across the back. Even a girl Brian barely knew drew a sun.

"Maybe this isn't so bad after all," Brian thought.

What nobody had warned him about was the itching.

It started in the second week and after that it never really stopped. It was not an ordinary itch on his skin, the sort you can deal with and forget. It was an itch underneath, somewhere in the middle of his arm, sealed away under a finger's thickness of hard plaster in a place no fingernail in the world could reach. He wriggled his fingers. He knocked the cast against his knee. He tried thinking about something else, which is the very worst plan, because an itch you are trying not to think about swells up until it is the only thing in your head.

In the end it was a ruler that saved him. The thin wooden one from his pencil case slid neatly down between the plaster and his arm, and if he worked it back and forth he could just reach the spot, and for a few seconds it was the finest feeling he had ever known. After that the ruler went everywhere with him.

Three weeks is a very long time when you are ten. Then, at last, they cut the cast open.

The arm that came out of it did not look like his arm. It was pale, a soft sickly white, the colour of something that had been shut in a cupboard and forgotten, and it looked thinner than the other one. All over it was a pasty grey film of old plaster and three weeks of skin, and it carried the smell of the inside of the cast with it.

But washing it was wonderful. He watched the grey come away under the water and his own proper arm appear again underneath. And then, best of all, he could scratch. With his own fingernails. Anywhere he liked, for as long as he liked. He stood there scratching his arm and thought that he had never in his whole life been so happy about anything so small.

And true to his word, that weekend Dad bought him the Super Soaker.

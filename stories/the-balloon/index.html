Kindergarten · ~1987

The Balloon

At the Mid-Autumn Festival, the whole family went to the Chinese Garden to see the lanterns. Mum and Dad, and Annie, and Brian.

It was one of those cool nights that Singapore hands out two or three times a year, and the garden was packed, and the whole place felt like a fair. There were lanterns strung along the paths and lanterns on the water, and enormous ones built up on frames as tall as a bus, a rabbit, a dragon, a pagoda, all of them glowing from the inside so that the light came through the paper in reds and yellows. Children went past with small ones on sticks. Somewhere ahead there was music.

Brian was five, and the trouble with being five in a crowd is that everything above you is other people's backs.

"Hold my hand," said Mum, about every twenty seconds.

At the gate Dad bought him a balloon, a blue one, which was his favourite colour, and tied the end of the string into a loop so that it would be easier to hold on to.

"There," Dad said. "Now we can find you. We only have to look for the balloon."

"What about me?" said Annie.

"You are tall enough."

Brian thought this was an excellent arrangement. He walked along under it, in and out of the crowd, checking every so often that it was still up there, a blue thing bobbing about above all those heads. When he could not see the lanterns for the people, he could always see his balloon.

Then the wind came up.

It came across the water in a gust that made everybody's clothes flap, and the lanterns swung on their wires, and the balloon went mad on the end of its string, jerking about over Brian's head. He held on. Mum reached over to steady him, and the string went across her, and the wind put it exactly where it should not have gone.

"Ouch," said Mum. She stopped walking. "Ouch, ouch."

The string had wound itself round her left earring.

Brian looked up and understood, in a cold rush, what he was holding. The string ran from the loop in his hand to his mother's ear. Every time the balloon pulled, it pulled on her. And the wind was still going, and the balloon was tugging and tugging.

So he let go.

He did not say anything about it. He simply opened his fingers and let the string sit loose in his hand, because he did not want to hurt her, and it did not once occur to him that this was information anybody else might need.

"Keep still," said Dad, leaning in. "Don't pull."

"I am not pulling."

"Not you. Anybody."

Mum stood very still with her head tilted over and her fingers working at the string. It was thin and slippery and knotted round the little gold post, and she could not see what she was doing because it was her own ear, and the wind kept snatching at the balloon while she worked.

"I cannot get it," she said at last. "It is properly caught. The quickest thing is to take the earring off, then we can untangle it after."

That sounded sensible to everybody. It sounded sensible to Brian too. He stood there and watched his mother reach up with both hands and undo the little gold catch behind her ear.

She lifted it clear.

And the balloon went.

There had been nothing holding it for some while now except Mum's earring, and the moment the earring came free it shot straight up, out of the crowd, over all the heads, with a small gold thing swinging on the end of the string.

Brian's mouth was open. Mum's hand was still up at her ear. Dad, who had been the one telling everybody to keep still, put his head back and watched it go with an expression Brian could not read at all.

"It's gone," said Annie.

Nobody moved. All four of them simply stood there in the middle of the crowd with their heads tipped back, watching, because there was nothing else that anybody could do about it.

"Never mind," Mum said, after a while. "It cannot be helped."

It went up past the lanterns. It cleared the pagoda and kept going, blue and getting smaller, out over the black water where the lights were reflected, and for a moment it was hard to tell which of the small bright things up there were lanterns and which one was Brian's balloon, carrying his mother's earring away with it into the middle of the sky.

Upper Primary · 1993

The Computer

It was simply there one day, on Dad's work table, where the soldering iron and the boxes of parts usually were.

There was a small television, and next to it a wide beige box lying flat, and Brian went straight over to look at the front of the box, because the front of the box was where everything was happening.

There were three round buttons in a row, and a little keyhole, and beside them a set of lights with words printed under them. POWER. TURBO. H.D.D. Above the buttons, on the right hand side, the box had slots in it: one narrow slot the size of a letterbox, one wider slot below it, and under those a long flat panel with two small buttons and a green light.

Brian read all the words twice and understood none of them and was already deeply impressed.

"That," said Dad, coming in behind him, "is a computer."

"What does it do?"

"Many things."

That was not an answer, and Dad knew it was not an answer, so he pulled out the chair and sat down and started showing him instead.

The wider slot took the big floppy disks, the ones in paper sleeves that really did flop. The narrow slot took the other kind, the small hard ones that clicked when they went in. The flat panel underneath was a CD-ROM drive, a single speed one, which Dad said was for a different sort of disk again.

"And," said Dad, "you can play games on it."

He pressed the power button.

The television, which was not a television, came on with a sort of thump, and white words went scrolling up the black screen faster than anybody could read them, and there was a beep, and then the words stopped and left a small blinking square sitting after a bit of writing that said C:\>.

Dad slid a floppy disk out of its paper sleeve, holding it by the edges, and pushed it into the wider slot and pulled the little lever down over it, and typed.

`a:`

The writing changed to A:\>. He typed something else. The drive made a noise like a small animal chewing, and the screen went black altogether for a second.

Then it said, in enormous letters, ARKANOID.

Brian did not sit down for the next ten minutes. It was a bar at the bottom of the screen that you slid left and right, and a ball that came down at it, and rows of coloured bricks at the top that broke and vanished one by one when the ball got up there, and every time the ball came off the bar it made a small sharp sound, like a coin dropping on a tile.

"Can I try?"

"Wait," said Dad, who was in the middle of a level.

After Arkanoid there was Galaga, where you controlled a little spaceship at the bottom and the aliens came down the screen in swarms, closer and closer, in formations that peeled off and dived at you. The early levels were manageable. Then, as they went on, the swarms came faster and from more directions at once, until there was a level where Brian lost all three of his ships in about forty seconds, and immediately wanted to go again.

Then Dad said there was one more thing, and took out a flat square case with a disk inside it that was not like a floppy disk at all. It was silver on one side, and when he tilted it the light came off it in rainbows.

"This is a CD," he said. "The whole encyclopaedia is on this."

Brian stopped.

He looked at the disk in Dad's hand. Then he looked across the living room at the bookshelf, where thirty large brown volumes stood in two rows with the gold still bright on their spines, taking up the better part of a wall.

"All of it? All of the brown ones? On that?"

"On that."

Brian did not entirely believe it, and said so. Dad did not argue with him. He pressed one of the small buttons on the flat panel instead, and a tray came sliding out of the front of the machine all by itself, which was the single most impressive thing Brian had seen a piece of furniture do in his whole life. Dad laid the disk into the tray, silver side down, and pressed the button again, and the tray took the disk back inside.

Then there was a whirring. It went on for a while, rising and settling and rising again, the sound of something spinning up somewhere inside the box.

Dad typed.

`d:`

Then a word Brian did not catch. And the screen filled up with a picture and a title, and it said Microsoft Encarta, and there was a box on it where you could type in whatever you wanted to know about.

"Try it," said Dad, and got up out of the chair.

So Brian sat down in front of it, at Dad's work table, in the chair that was too high for him, and thought about what to ask a machine that had the world in it. He could have asked it anything. That was the whole point of it, and it was almost too much to choose from.

He typed Africa, because a year ago, in the June holidays, that was where he had started.

And the machine thought about it for a moment, with the disk whirring away inside the beige box, and then it gave him Africa: the deserts at the top and the forests in the middle, exactly as the white books had, and a map, and photographs in colour, and, when he clicked on a small speaker at the corner of the picture, drumming came out of the machine and filled the whole room.

Brian sat quite still and listened to all of it with his mouth slightly open.

Then he turned round in the chair.

"Dad. Can we play Galaga again?"

So Dad pressed the button, and the tray came out, and the entire knowledge of the world went back into its flat square case on the corner of the work table. The floppy disk went into A:. And the two of them sat there until Mum called them to eat, moving a small ship left and right along the bottom of the screen while the aliens came down at them in their swarms.

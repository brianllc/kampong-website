Upper Primary

Half an Eraser

<!-- status: awaiting read · level: P4 · year: 1992 -->

Yueming knew exactly why Brian had been put to sit next to her. Neither of them ever said it out loud, and that was the arrangement for the first few months. He was the boy who had come last in Chinese. She was the girl who came first, and not by a little.

Earlier that year Brian watched her walk up onto the stage at assembly to recite a Chinese essay in front of the whole level. She did it without a paper in her hand and recited perfectly, in the right order and with the right pauses, while more than three hundred children sat on the floor and listened. Brian sat on the floor with them and thought about the fact that he could not reliably finish a single sentence in proper Chinese.

So for the first month or so they simply worked alongside each other, like two people waiting at the same bus stop for different buses. When they did speak, it sometimes felt like a chicken talking to a duck.

It went on like that until the examination.

It was a Mathematics paper, and the first section was multiple choice, which meant an optical answer sheet. You took your 2B pencil and shaded in a small oval beside A, B, C or D, filling it right up to the edges, and a machine somewhere read it afterwards and scored your answer.

Brian was halfway down the sheet when he noticed that Yueming was acting strangely.

She was going frantically through her pencil case, and then she stopped, and then she went through it again, and then she just sat still. Brian stole a quick glance and knew what was missing. An eraser, which meant bad news, because an eraser was not optional for an optical answer sheet. If you changed your mind you had to rub the oval out completely, until the oval was white again.

Yueming looked up towards the front, where Zhuo Laoshi sat marking something, and she did not put her hand up. Brian understood that too. The rule in an examination was silence, and at nine years old a rule is not something you weigh up against your own difficulty. It is simply a thing you do not break. Putting her hand up would have made her the one who broke it, in front of everybody.

He had one eraser. It was old, and worn down to about the length of a thumbnail, and he needed it just as much as anyone in that room.

He took his ruler and sawed at it under the desk until it split.

Then he rolled one half across the join onto Yueming's side of the desk.

There was a pause. Then the half eraser was gone.

They said nothing when the paper ended. But when they came out into the corridor afterwards, Yueming stopped and turned round.

"Thank you," she said. "I really thought I was finished."

That was all it took. Nine years old is nine years old, and once somebody has said thank you to you in a corridor there is no longer any reason not to talk to them.

She started helping him with Chinese, and she did it without remarking on how little he knew. She would look at what he had written and say that the bottom of that one was wrong, and draw it in his margin stroke by stroke, and make him write it three times.

And it turned out Brian had something to trade. Chinese was the one subject he was bad at. His English was good, his Mathematics was good, and Science he actually liked, which was more than Yueming could say for any of them. So the traffic began to go both ways across that desk. He explained why the ice floated in her drink, and why two magnets would pull together one way round and push apart the other, and she took her turn at being the one who did not know, and neither of them remarked on that either.

What she liked best were riddles.

"What is yours," she said one afternoon, "but other people use it more than you do?"

"Your name," said Brian, without looking up.

"Try this one. You can have a watch that is correct twice a day, or a watch that is always five minutes slow. Which do you take?"

"The one that is five minutes slow," said Brian. "The other one is stopped."

Yueming looked at him for a moment.

"You are not supposed to be good at this," she said.

She could not get past him with the English ones, so she changed the game.

Then one afternoon she wrote something in the corner of her exercise book and turned it towards him.

一月一日非今天

"A moon, a sun, and not today," she said. "What is it?"

Brian looked at it for a long while. He knew 月 and he knew 日, because everybody knew those two, but he had no idea what the riddle meant.

Yueming covered up 非今天 with one hand and pointed at the 日, and then at the 月, and then pushed the two of them together with her finger.

明.

"Ming," she said. "And not today. So?"

"明天," said Brian slowly. "Tomorrow."

"Correct."

That was how it went from then on. They traded lessons and riddles, and the Chinese comics were doing their work by then as well, and between the comics and the top girl in Chinese the fences slowly came down.

At the end of Primary Four the streaming results came. Brian was placed into EM2.

Yueming was placed into EM1.

They were in different classes after that. Brian still had his half of the eraser in his pencil case, and he went on using it until there was nothing left of it to use.

Upper Primary

The 175

<!-- status: awaiting read · level: P5 · year: 1993 -->

There were five of them, and it was the bus that did it.

Derrick, Brian, Shunlai, Weijia and Yehao were in the same class, which is how they knew each other, but being in the same class only gets you so far. What made them friends was that all five of them lived out at Teck Whye or Choa Chu Kang, which meant that every single afternoon, when everybody else had gone off in their own directions, the five of them ended up standing at the same bus stop waiting for the same bus.

It was the 175. It is called the 975 now, and has been for years, but in those days it was the 175 and it came when it felt like coming.

You cannot stand about with four other boys every day for a year and not become close. So they did.

They were all reading comics that year, and one of the ones they liked best was Dodge Danpei, about a small round boy who threw a dodgeball so hard it seemed to bend the air. It had everything in it. The action was fast, the moves had names, and people were forever being knocked off their feet by a ball travelling faster than any ball has ever travelled.

There was only one problem with Dodge Danpei, which was that nobody in Singapore played dodgeball.

It was not in their PE lessons. It was not a thing anybody's older brother did. As far as the five of them could establish, the entire game existed in a Japanese comic and nowhere else in the world, and if they wanted to play it they were going to have to work out how from the drawings.

So they did that too.

The first attempt used a basketball, because a basketball was what Weijia could get hold of.

"Ready?" said Weijia.

"Ready," said Shunlai, who was not.

It hit him in the ribs and folded him in half, and everybody stood around him for a while being sympathetic and trying not to laugh. Basketballs, it turned out, are extremely heavy. They are also too big to hold in one hand, which meant nobody could throw one the way Danpei threw, with the wrist snapping over at the last moment.

A soccer ball was no better. Lighter, but just as wide, and after it caught Yehao on the side of the face they retired that one too.

"Aiyah," said Yehao, with his hand over his eye. "How he do it in the comic?"

"He is drawn," said Brian.

Then one afternoon Derrick turned up with a ball he had found somewhere, and never would say where. It was a bit smaller than a soccer ball and it was made of some sort of spongy foam, light enough to hold in one hand and squeeze, and when you threw it at somebody it hit them and did nothing at all.

It was, for their purposes, perfect.

The other problem was that there were five of them, and dodgeball wants two proper teams with a good number on each side. Five will not divide. So they invented a version that fitted the boys they actually had, which is what children do, and they argued the rules out on the pavement over about a week.

Two against two. Whoever was left over stood behind and chased the ball, because a dodged ball goes a long way and somebody has to go and get it, and if it was going to be anybody it might as well be somebody with a job. You took turns being the one at the back. Nobody had to be told that this was fair.

After that they played every afternoon.

It was not really at the bus stop. It was in the void deck just along from it, on Petir Road, where there were pillars to hide behind and a concrete floor and shade, and where five boys with their school bags in a heap could carry on for as long as they liked without anybody minding very much. They played in their uniforms. They played with their shirts coming out. And all the time, one of them would be half looking down the road, because the whole thing ran on the bus.

"Bus!"

And the game would stop exactly where it was, mid-throw, and five boys would fall on the pile of bags and sort out whose was whose and run for the stop with the ball under somebody's arm, arriving in a line at the door as though they had been standing there patiently the entire time.

Once or twice they did not see it in time.

They would be arguing about whether a ball had touched somebody's shoe or only their sock, and the 175 would pull in behind them and let its passengers off and take on the ones who were waiting, and pull out again, and by the time anybody looked up it was already down the road.

Nobody minded. There would be another one. So the five of them sat on the low wall by the stop with the ball in somebody's lap, sweating through their uniforms in the late afternoon heat, waiting for the next 175 and getting their breath back from a game that did not exist anywhere in Singapore except in a comic and in a void deck on Petir Road.

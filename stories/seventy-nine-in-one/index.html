Lower Primary

Seventy-Nine in One

<!-- status: awaiting read · level: P3 · year: 1991 -->

Brian had seen the games on a small television in a shop in the neighbourhood, the sort of shop that sold radios and cassette players and had a glass counter at the back. He had gone and stood outside that shop more times than was reasonable, watching the owner play on the screen with a small white and red box wired up in front of it. It was a Nintendo.

Brian wanted one badly, but Dad said the real cost was not the machine. The machine was useless on its own, and every game after that was another cartridge and another price.

"There is a cartridge," he told Dad. "It has seventy-nine games on it."

Dad looked up.

"Seventy-nine."

"On one cartridge. So you only buy it the one time."

"And then?"

"And then that is all the games I will ever need," said Brian.

He meant it, too. He had thought it through and seventy-nine was a large number. Seventy-nine was more games than there were days in the school holidays. A boy who had seventy-nine games could play a new game every day of the school holiday and yet would not finish them.

Dad did not say yes and he did not say no, which is a thing parents do. Then one day they went and bought it.

Brian carried the Nintendo home himself, with the cartridge as well.

The cartridge was bright yellow, and heavier than it looked. The label across the front of it was blue and glossy and extremely busy. 79 in 1 ran across the top in fat yellow numerals, and under that, in smaller white type, NT-029, and beside them a pink oval with Chinese characters in it that Brian could not read. Crowded round the numbers were pictures of what was supposed to be inside: Mario, a racing car, a fighter plane coming up a canyon, a bee with a face. And then, in among them, photographs. An actual photograph of a man playing tennis. An actual photograph of a man leaning over a snooker table, and another of a man in a cap holding a fishing rod, as though somebody had run out of games to draw and gone and found some grown-ups instead.

Not one game on that cartridge ever looked anything like the photographs. Brian did not mind in the least.

They set it up on the television in the living room. Dad did the wires, and Brian stood beside him holding the cartridge and being no help whatsoever. Then the cartridge went into the slot, and it went down with a solid click.

The screen came up with a list. Not pictures. A list, in a plain white typeface on a blue background, numbered from one, and you moved down it with the pad and pressed the red button on the game you wanted.

He started at the top and worked down.

There was Super Mario, of course, which everybody knew. There was Excitebike, where the motorcycle overheated if you held the fast button too long, which Brian did every single time. There was Donkey Kong, and the barrels. There was Ice Climber, with the two little men in parkas going up through the ice with hammers. There was Antarctic Adventure, where you were a penguin, and Circus Charlie, where you jumped a lion through a ring of fire, and Kung Fu, and Dig Dug, and Contra, and Bomberman, and games of baseball and tennis and golf, and Othello, where Brian worked out after a while that the whole thing was to take the four corners, and Macross, and more after that, and more again.

Some of the games took two players, and on those Annie would sit down beside him and take the second pad. She was not really interested in any of it. She would play tennis for a while, or a few rounds of Bomberman, and be perfectly good at it, and then get up and go and do something else, in the way of a person who has been humouring somebody.

So most days it was the single player games, on his own.

Cherie was far too small and mainly wanted to press the buttons, so she was given the second pad, which in most of the games did nothing whatever, and she would press away at it happily and do no harm to anybody. But the flat in the afternoon, after homework, with the television to himself and seventy-nine games sitting in a list waiting to be worked through, was as much as Brian required of a day.

He would sit on the floor with the pad in both hands, close enough that Mum told him to move back, and he would move back, and then over the next hour he would move forward again without noticing.

"Have you done your homework?"

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Yes."

Which is how Brian came to be, that year, the fastest boy in the flat at finishing his homework.

Then he found out that Andrew had one too.

Andrew was in his class and lived in the next block along, and their mothers knew each other, which meant that going to Andrew's was not a thing that had to be arranged by anybody. It simply came out in conversation one day, the way these things do, and after that Andrew came over to the flat on some afternoons and Brian went over to Andrew's on others. Andrew did not have a seventy-nine in one. Andrew had ordinary cartridges, one game on each, the newer sort that were too big to sit alongside anything else, and he had three of them.

He had Chip 'n Dale Rescue Rangers, where you were a chipmunk who went round picking up crates and throwing them at things, and which took two players at once, so both of them could be on it together. He had Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles, off the cartoon. And he had Mega Man, where you were a small blue robot, and every time you beat one of the bosses you took his weapon off him and used it against the next one, which Brian considered the finest idea anybody had ever had.

Not one of the three was on the seventy-nine in one.

Meanwhile Brian's list was getting shorter.

Not the list on the screen, which stayed at seventy-nine. The list in his head, of games he had not finished. They fell away one by one. He knew where every barrel was going and where every alien came from and the exact moment the motorcycle would begin to smoke. Super Mario took the longest, because Super Mario had eight worlds in it and a princess at the end, and every time he reached the end of a castle a small man in a mushroom hat told him she was in another one. But he got to all of them eventually.

And then one afternoon Brian sat on the floor in front of a television with seventy-nine games on it and could not think of a single one of them he wanted to play again.

It was Andrew who gave him the idea, without meaning to, simply by owning three cartridges Brian had never played. Brian worked it out on the way home and could hardly sit still.

He had seventy-nine games. Andrew had three. If he lent Andrew the seventy-nine, and Andrew lent him the three, then for as long as the swap lasted Brian would have three new games and Andrew would have seventy-nine, and neither of them would have spent a cent.

He suggested it to Andrew the next morning and Andrew agreed before Brian had finished explaining.

That evening Mum came past the living room and stopped.

"Where did that one come from?"

Brian's heart went straight up into his throat.

He had not thought, until that exact second, about whether he had been allowed to do it. The cartridge had been bought for him, which he was fairly certain made it his. It had also been bought with Dad's money, which he was fairly certain made it not entirely his. And he had taken it out of the flat and handed it to somebody without asking a single adult about any of it.

"I swapped with Andrew," he said, very fast. "Only for a while. He has got mine and I have got his and we are swapping back after."

"Mm," said Mum.

And she went off to finish what she had been doing, because it was an evening like any other evening and there was still a great deal of it left to get through.

Brian sat back down on the floor and picked up the pad, and after a while his heart came back down to where it lived, and he started a game he had never played before.

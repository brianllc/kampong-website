Kindergarten · ~1987

Mopping

When Mum was pregnant, she could not bend down any more.

This turned out to be a serious problem, because a great deal of housework happens near the floor. She could still cook and she could still fold, but she could not get down to the tiles, and there was a flat to keep clean.

So one morning she made an announcement.

"From now on," said Mum, "if you do the housework, I will pay you."

Brian and Annie looked at each other.

"Mopping the floor, ten cents," said Mum. "Sweeping, ten cents. Wiping down the table, five cents. Putting the shoes straight, five cents. Folding the laundry, ten cents."

Brian did not know very much arithmetic yet, but he knew the important part of it, which was that up to that morning, money had been something that turned up now and then, from grandparents, at festivals, for no reason you could rely on. Now it turned out that money could be obtained.

There was a mama shop directly below the block, and everything in it was stocked for children - marbles, kites, ice-cream, snacks and cold drinks. What Brian wanted from it was Bee Bee: a packet of dry crunchy noodle bits with a little bit of seasoning powder inside, which you tipped in and shook up and then ate straight out of the bag with your fingers.

After that the two of them fought over the housework like dogs over a bone.

"I am wiping the table."

"You wiped it yesterday."

"Then you sweep the floor."

"I don't want to sweep the floor."

Nobody in that flat has ever been so keen to work. Mum would name a job and two children would materialise.

But the one Brian wanted, every time, was mopping the floor.

There was no mop in the house. There was a rag and there was a pail, and the way it was done was that you soaked the rag, wrung it out, got down on your hands and knees, and worked your way backwards across the room. That was the way Mum had always done it before her stomach got in the way.

That was not quite the way Brian did it.

What Brian did first was fetch the pail and pour water across the marble floor. Not a little. A generous, sloshing, unnecessary amount, spread about with the rag until the whole living room shone with it and you could see the ceiling light lying on the surface.

Then he backed up to the far wall, got down on all fours, and pushed off.

"Wheeeee."

He went the entire length of the living room on his knees. The wet marble was as slippery as anything he had ever been on, better than the slide at the playground because the slide only went one way and this went everywhere, and he could steer a bit with his hands, and if he pushed hard enough he could get all the way across and finish with his fingers on the skirting board at the other side.

And when he got there he did not stand up. He set both hands flat against the wall, gathered himself up, and shoved off backwards, and away he went down the living room the other way.

It was exactly how you swim lengths in a swimming pool, turning at the wall and pushing off it to go back. Brian had never in his life been in a swimming pool. He had worked the whole thing out for himself, on his knees, on a wet floor.

He did it until his knees were pink and stinging, and only then, with everything soaked and nothing whatsoever accomplished, did he pick up the rag and begin the actual job. Wring, wipe, wring, wipe, backwards across the floor, until the floor came up dry and clean and there was no evidence at all.

Mum inspected it from the sofa, which was as far as she could easily get.

"Very good," she said. "The floor is very clean today."

"Thank you," said Brian.

He collected his money and went downstairs on two pink knees, walking slightly oddly, and bought himself a packet of Bee Bee.

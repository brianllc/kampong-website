Upper Primary · ~1992

Getting Better in Chinese, Part 1

Brian was the worst in his class at Chinese, and everybody knew it, including Brian.

At home, Mum and Dad spoke only English. Chinese lived inside the classroom and nowhere else, so while his classmates chattered away, Brian sat looking at the characters in his textbook as if they were a wall of little fences with far too many sticks. No matter how long he stared, the sticks would not turn themselves into words.

There were forty-one pupils in the class. When the term results were read out, Brian came last of all, forty-first. The two boys just ahead of him, in thirty-ninth and fortieth place, were Zakir and Ahmad, and both of them had chosen to take Chinese as their second language when they could have taken Malay. Brian was the only Chinese boy he knew who could be beaten at his own mother tongue by his Malay friends.

One morning, Zhuo Laoshi returned the quarterly test papers. She walked down the rows and laid each one face down on the desks. Brian turned his over slowly. Red ink everywhere, and a number at the top he did not want to look at twice.

"How many you got?" whispered Shunlai, his deskmate.

"Don't ask," said Brian. "You?"

Shunlai only made a funny face to cheer Brian up, and the two of them tried not to laugh so that Zhuo Laoshi would not hear.

It seemed to Brian that no matter how hard he tried, the wall of fences would never come down.

Then, one afternoon, Shunlai slid a small book across the desk.

"Eh, read this," he whispered. "My favourite!"

It was a 七龙珠 comic, Dragon Ball. Brian opened it, and on the very first page a small boy with a monkey's tail came flying across the panel on a little yellow cloud, grinning all the way. There were many words in each speech bubble, but the pictures did most of the work, and Brian found he could follow the story from the drawings alone. Whenever he matched a character he knew to the picture beside it, the meaning clicked quietly into place.

He read it once. Then he read it again and again.

That weekend he went down to the shop at the corner of Block 26. It was one of those neighbourhood shops that sold a bit of everything, exercise books and foolscap paper and pencil cases and rulers, and the comics lived outside on a display shelf by the door, standing in rows with their covers facing out. A man sat out there beside a table of newspapers, and it was to him that you paid.

Brian stood in front of that shelf for a long time.

He was not looking for the book Shunlai had lent him. He already knew how that one ended. What he wanted was to find out what happened next to the boy on the yellow cloud. So he went along the row until he found the volume that came after it, and he took it down.

Then he paid the man at the newspaper table with his own pocket money, counted out of his hand and onto the wood.

He did not think anything of it at the time. But it was the first time in his life that Brian had ever spent his own money on something written in Chinese.

Bit by bit, something was happening that Brian did not quite notice. The characters in the bubbles began to feel less like fences and more like familiar faces. Some words he had read in Dragon Ball on Monday would turn up in his textbook on Wednesday, and there they would be, old friends in a strange place. 朋友. 一起. 大家. He could read those now. He had not studied them. He had simply met them so many times that they had become his friends.

The next quarterly test, Brian was not last. He was not near the top either, not by a long way. But when Zhuo Laoshi read out the results, the name right at the bottom was somebody else's, and Brian sat up a little straighter.

One afternoon Zhuo Laoshi stopped at his desk. Brian was meant to be doing his maths, but a Dragon Ball comic lay open on his knees under the table.

"Brian," said Zhuo Laoshi. "Is that Chinese?"

"Yes, Laoshi," said Brian, bracing himself for trouble.

Zhuo Laoshi looked at the cover for a long moment, at Wukong caught mid-leap with his fist drawn back. Then she did something Brian had never thought she would do.

"Carry on," she said, and walked away.

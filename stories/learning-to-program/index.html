Upper Primary · 1994

Learning to Program

The computer sat in the corner of the flat, and as far as Brian was concerned it was a machine for playing games on. That was what it was for. You switched it on, you waited, you played.

Zhiyuan knew it was for something else.

Nobody had taught him. Computers were a rare thing to have in a flat in those days, and there was no class for it and no teacher and no club, and his parents had not sat him down and shown him. He had simply decided at some point that he wanted to know how the thing worked, and gone and found out by himself, the way he found out about everything, and by the time he was eleven he could make a computer do things.

Brian, at eleven, knew the commands that started the games. That was the whole of it.

So one afternoon, when Zhiyuan came over, he sat himself down in front of the screen without asking.

"Eh, watch this," he said.

"Which game?"

"No game."

The screen went black and empty, with white letters in the top corner and a small square cursor blinking away to itself. Brian had seen this screen a hundred times. It was the boring part, the part where you typed your way through to the games folder so the good part could start.

Zhiyuan typed something. Brian was not really watching what.

Then he pressed a key, and the screen said:

Your computer has been infected by a virus.

Everything in Brian went cold.

"What did you do," he said. "What did you do."

"Wait."

"My father is going to kill me."

He was not exaggerating, or he did not think he was. He had no idea how you fixed a virus, or whether a virus could be fixed at all, and the words just sat there on the screen, patient and terrible, not going away.

Beside him, Zhiyuan was laughing so hard he had to hold on to the table.

"Don't worry lah," he said, when he could speak. "I only asked the computer to print out those words."

Brian looked at him. Then he looked at the screen. Then he looked at him again.

"You what?"

"Print. You type print, then you put whatever you want inside the quote marks, then you run it. The computer just says it."

"Anything?"

"Anything."

"Say it again."

"Anything," said Zhiyuan. "It doesn't know what the words mean. It only knows you told it to say them."

Brian sat back and looked at the sentence still sitting there on the black screen.

The computer had not been infected by anything. The computer had been told to say that it had, and the computer, having no opinion of its own, had said it. It did not know whether the words were true, and it did not care, and it would have put them up on the screen in exactly the same way either way.

"Move," said Brian. "Let me try."

He printed his own name. He printed a few other things, just to watch them appear. Then he typed out Your computer has been infected by a virus himself, to be quite sure it came up exactly the same as before, and sat looking at it for a moment, and cleared the screen.

Most schoolboys are taught to make a computer say hello, world. Brian's first program told him his computer had been infected, and he learnt what print meant while sitting there convinced he had ruined the most expensive thing in the flat, which he maintains is a far more memorable way to learn it.

For a good while after that it stayed a party trick and not much more. He would bring his sister in to look at the screen, or a neighbour, or somebody from school, and watch their face while it announced something alarming.

"Your computer spoilt already," said Shunlai, the first time.

"No," said Brian. "I told it to say that."

"Then tell it to say I am handsome."

So Brian told it to say that Shunlai was handsome, and the computer, which had no opinion about that either, said so.

Then Brian and Zhiyuan put the games back on and went back to playing them, because they were eleven, and the games were very good.

But Brian and Zhiyuan both, in the end, went on to tell computers what to say for a living. Neither of them ever quite got over the discovery that you could.

Upper Primary · 1992

The Encyclopaedia

A few days before the June holidays, a man knocked on the door in the evening, and Dad went to answer it, and Brian went and stood behind Dad to see who it was.

The man was not selling insurance and he was not collecting for anything. He had a brochure. He held it open with both hands, the way you hold something you are proud of, and the pages were thick and shiny and had photographs on them of shelves full of books.

"These books," said the man, "contain the knowledge of the world."

Brian, who was nine, thought that was an extremely large claim, and leaned out from behind Dad to look at the brochure.

They stood at the door a long time. Dad asked questions of the sort Dad asked, about how many volumes there were and how often they were brought up to date, and the man had answers for all of them, and at some point Mum came to see what was taking so long and stayed to listen too. Brian went back inside eventually and forgot about it.

A week later the books came.

They came up the four flights in loads, in the arms of two men who had to stop on the landings, and there were far more of them than Brian had understood from the brochure. There was also a bookshelf, because a delivery like that assumes you do not have anywhere to put it, and the bookshelf came up in pieces and went together against the wall of the living room.

Then they filled it.

Thirty large brown volumes, all the same, with Britannica on the spines in gold. They took up two whole shelves and they made the living room look like somewhere else entirely, somewhere serious, a room where important things were decided.

Below them went a row of thinner white ones. Children's Encyclopaedia, those said.

And on the bottom shelf, in a box, sat four books called the PSLE Preparation Guide, one each for English, Chinese, Mathematics and Science.

Brian read the word PSLE. He knew what it was. It was the thing at the end of Primary Six that made people go strange, and he was in Primary Four, so his turn was two years off, which at nine years old is roughly the same as never. He put the box back where he had found it.

Those four books stayed on the bottom shelf, untouched, for a long time. Brian would come back to them in the end, but not for two years, and not because he wanted to.

When it was all in, Dad stood in front of the shelf with his hands on his hips and looked at it with great satisfaction.

"Everything is in there," he said. "The knowledge of the world. If you children finish reading all of that, you will be really smart."

"All of it?"

"All of it."

Brian looked at the two brown shelves. Annie looked at them too.

That evening he took down one of the brown books, because he had decided to become really smart, and it seemed sensible to start at once.

He got it open on the floor and knelt over it.

The pages were enormous and thin, like the pages of a Bible, and each one was in two columns, and the words in the columns were tiny. Brian put his face closer. The words were not only tiny, they were also, when he tried a few of them, not words that led anywhere. Every sentence seemed to be about six other things he had never heard of, and it did not stop and explain any of them, and it plainly had no intention of ever stopping and explaining any of them. It was not written for him. It was not written for anyone he had ever met.

He read most of a page, understood almost none of it, and closed the book with the very quiet feeling of having been defeated by furniture.

Then he tried a white one.

The white ones were a different thing altogether. The print was bigger. There were pictures on nearly every page, in colour, and the pictures had captions under them, and the writing between the pictures was written as if somebody had actually thought about a person reading it.

He had picked up A, because A came first.

So Brian spent that evening on the floor of the living room learning about Africa, which was enormous and had deserts at the top and forests in the middle. Then about the Aborigines of Australia, who had been there for a very long time before anybody else arrived. Then Aesop, who told a story about a tortoise and a hare, and another about a fox who decided he had not wanted the grapes anyway. Then Agriculture, which was more interesting than it sounded, and had a picture of a plough.

"What are you reading?"

"A," said Brian.

That was the June holidays gone. He read A, and then he read the next one, and kept going, and when he reached the end he found that he did not mind starting again, because a great deal of it had already gone quietly out of his head and it was just as good the second time. He read them through several times over. He learned the flags of countries he could not have found on a map, and how a volcano works, and what the inside of a castle looked like, and the names of dinosaurs, and every so often he would arrive at the dinner table with a fact and put it down in front of everybody like a cat bringing in something from the garden.

He never did open the brown ones again.

By the end of the year you could tell the two halves of that bookshelf apart from across the room. The white books had gone soft at the corners and grubby along the bottom edge where his thumbs went, and one of them had lost its spine and been mended with tape.

Above them, in a row, thirty brown volumes stood with the gold still bright on them, holding the knowledge of the world, exactly as new as the day the two men carried them up the stairs.

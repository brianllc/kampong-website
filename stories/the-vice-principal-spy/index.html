Lower Primary · 1991

The Vice-Principal Spy

In Primary Three the school got a new vice-principal, and Brian spent the better part of a year investigating him.

It started at assembly. The whole school was out on the parade square in the morning sun, and the principal said that this was Mr Lim, who had joined them, and a man came forward and said good morning to nine hundred children, and nine hundred children said good morning back.

Brian did not say it back. He was busy looking.

Because the man at the front, standing with his hands behind him and his shirt tucked in very neatly, looked like Dad.

Not exactly like Dad. That was the whole trouble. If he had looked exactly like Dad the matter would have been simple and Brian would have gone home and asked about it. Instead he looked like Dad the way a photograph of Dad taken from the wrong side might look like Dad: the same build, the same way of standing, something about the face that Brian recognised without being able to say which part of it he was recognising. He was and he was not.

Then, a few days later, Brian saw the name written down.

It was on a notice, and it said Mr Lim Ngap Seng.

Brian stood in the corridor and read it several times.

Dad's name was Lim Ngap Sing. Brian knew that the way you know a thing that is written on forms, and here it was on the school notice board with one letter changed, one single letter out of eleven, which is not the sort of thing that happens by accident.

That was when the investigation properly began.

The theory was this. Dad was an engineer, and engineers went out in the morning and came back in the evening, and nobody in the family had ever actually followed him to see where he went in between. Suppose he did not go to work at all. Suppose he went to Brian's school instead, and put on a different shirt, and changed one letter of his name, and spent the day walking up and down the corridors with his hands behind his back finding out exactly what his son was up to. It would explain a great deal. It would certainly explain how much Dad seemed to know.

Brian did not tell anybody this for a long time. Then one recess he told Weijia, who took it seriously, because Weijia took most things seriously.

"Your father is the vice-principal?"

"I said he might be."

"Then who is at his office?"

"Nobody. That's the point."

Weijia thought about it. "Then you better don't do anything wrong," he said.

After that, Brian watched the vice-principal every chance he got.

He watched him at assembly, from the third row, checking the ears, because Brian had read somewhere that ears were the part of a person that could not be disguised. He watched him come along the upper corridor, and made himself walk past at exactly the right moment, and looked hard at his shoes. He listened to his voice when he spoke over the microphone, and could not decide, because a microphone makes everybody sound like somebody else. Once the vice-principal caught him staring and said, "Yes? What is it?" and Brian said, "Nothing, sir," and went away very fast with his heart going.

At home he ran his tests over dinner.

"Dad, what did you do today?"

"Work."

"What work?"

"Drawings," said Dad, who was an engineer and gave engineer's answers.

Which proved nothing at all, in either direction, and Brian went on eating.

It went on like that for months. And then it ended the way these things usually end, which is to say in about four seconds, without any warning, from the wrong direction entirely.

It was a meet-the-parents session, and Mum had come to the school, and they were walking along the corridor together when the vice-principal went past and nodded to them both.

Mum stopped.

"Brian!" she said. "He's your father's cousin. Didn't you know?"

Brian did not know.

Nobody had ever told him, because there had never been an occasion to tell him. Dad's side of the family was not a close one. There were no big dinners with that half of the family, no visiting at Chinese New Year, no aunties pinching his cheek and telling him how tall he had got. They were people who existed, the way a country you have not been to exists. And so it turned out that a man Brian had been examining for months at four hundred paces was a relative, and had been all along, and had presumably known perfectly well who Brian was every time he walked past him.

"Cousin," said Brian.

"Cousin," said Mum, and went in to see the teacher.

At assembly the next morning Brian stood in the third row and looked at the vice-principal properly, without any of the investigating, and found that he could suddenly see it: not Dad wearing a different shirt, but a man with Dad's build and Dad's way of standing and half of Dad's face, being himself, at the front of a school, with his own name.

And for the rest of his time at that school, Brian never once had to worry that his father was in the building.

Lower Primary · ~1988

Learning to Read

The year before Brian started Primary One, Mum began to worry.

The trouble was that he could not read. Not properly. He knew some letters and he could get through the front of a book if he already knew what it said, but put a new sentence in front of him and he was guessing, and everybody could tell he was guessing, including Brian.

He was not worried about it himself. He was small, and there was a playground downstairs, and there were toys on the floor of the bedroom, and reading was a thing that grown-ups did with the newspaper. But Primary One was coming, and Mum could see it coming, and she had decided that her son was not going to walk in there unable to read.

So one afternoon she cleared the table.

She put a book on it. It was a Ladybird book, small and square and hard-backed, and it was The Three Little Pigs.

And beside the book she put the cane.

"Read," said Mum.

Brian read.

"Once upon a time there were three little pigs."

That much he could do, because everybody knows how it starts. Then the sentence went on, and there was a word in it he had never seen before, and he looked at it and had a go.

"Saw."

"No."

"Sore."

The cane came down once across the back of his hand, quick and stinging, and Brian's eyes went hot, and he did not look up.

"Straw," said Mum. "Say it."

"Straw."

"Again."

"Straw."

"Good. Go on from there."

So he went on from there. That was how it went, word by word along the line with his finger underneath the sentence. Every word he got wrong cost him one stroke. Every stroke was over quickly. And after each one Mum said the word properly and made him say it back, and then made him carry on from where he was.

And here is the thing that Brian remembers about it, thirty years later. It worked.

The words that stung were the words he did not forget. The next time that word came round he saw it coming from halfway down the line, and his mouth was already ready for it, and he said it right. Whatever the cane was doing, it was driving each word in and fixing it there, and the words he never got wrong were the ones he could not have told you how he had learnt.

The first afternoon was a long one.

But it was not many afternoons before he sat at that table and read The Three Little Pigs from the first page to the last with his finger going along under the words, and got to the end of it, and there had not been a single stroke.

Mum did not make a fuss about it. She took the book away and put down the next one.

Then it was that one. Then the one after.

And it turned out that once the machine was running there was no stopping it, because the third book was easier than the second and the fourth was easier than the third. Somewhere in there the cane stopped being needed and simply lay on the table, and then it stopped being on the table at all.

He went through the whole Ladybird series that way.

By the end of it Brian had not yet spent a single day in Primary One, and could read anything anybody put in front of him, and had begun, when nobody was watching, to read things nobody had put in front of him at all.

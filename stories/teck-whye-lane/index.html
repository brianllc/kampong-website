Kindergarten · ~1986

Teck Whye Lane

Teck Whye Lane ran along a little hill, and Block 15 stood at the top of it, and Brian lived on the fourth floor.

The fourth floor was exactly the right height for a family. It was high enough that the flat caught whatever wind was going, especially since they were on a hill, and low enough that if you stood at the living room window you could see the whole playground below, and tell one small child from another, and know which of them was yours.

This turned out to matter a great deal.

Inside, the flat had three bedrooms and a storeroom. The storeroom was dark and packed to the ceiling with things nobody had looked at in years, and it was the most interesting room in the house for exactly that reason. Brian and Annie shared one of the bedrooms in those days, which is how it usually goes when you are small, and at that age Brian could not imagine any other arrangement.

There was only one other flat on their landing, and the family in it had three daughters.

The three girls were teenagers, which to Brian meant that they were adults who were allowed to be interesting. They always treated both Brian and Annie well, being like older sisters to them.

"Wah, so big already," one of them would say, whenever she saw him, which she said every single time, including the times she had seen him the day before.

Brian never knew what to say back, so he said nothing and looked away shyly.

In the mornings, before he was old enough for kindergarten, Brian had the whole flat and the whole day as his sister was at school.

Mum was always there, and always doing something. She cooked, and she cleaned, and while she did it the radio was on, so the flat came with a permanent low sound in it, songs and talking and more songs, coming from the kitchen. Brian played on the floor with his toys, or lay on his stomach with a book, and the radio went on above him, and Mum went past every so often carrying something.

Late in the morning, the two of them went down to fetch Annie.

The kindergarten was at the bottom of the hill, close enough to walk to, and walking down was no trouble at all. Mum held his hand for the part with the road in it and let go for the rest, and Brian would get to the gate first and stand there waiting with his fingers through the wire.

Then the doors would open and a great many small children would come out at once, all of them in the same uniform, and Brian would go along the line of them looking for the one that was his.

"There," he would say. "There she is."

Annie always came out talking. She talked about her teacher and about somebody who had cried and about what they had been given to eat, and she talked the whole way home, and Brian listened to every word of it, because kindergarten was a foreign country that he had not been to yet and she had been there all morning.

Then there was the hill.

Going down had been nothing. Going up was a different matter, in the late morning, with the sun on the back of your neck, and by the time they reached the block Brian would be dragging along about four steps behind, and Mum would say what she said every single day, which was that he had run down it quite happily an hour ago.

Then in the afternoon, when the worst of it was done, she would sit down.

That was the good part of the day. She would come out with a drink and put the television on, and Brian would come and sit with her and watch whatever was on for an hour or so. He did not always understand the programme. It did not matter. What mattered was being able to watch TV.

Then, when the heat went out of the afternoon, he went down.

Down four floors, past the neighbours' door, and out into the whole world.

The world was not large. It was one playground, one long dirt patch off to the left where the older boys fought over marbles, a stretch of grass to the right with jackfruit trees and starfruit trees growing on it, and a mama shop under the block that sold everything a child could want and nothing a child could afford. That was it. That was the entire world, and it was more than enough of one, and Brian knew every corner of it the way you know your own hands.

He stayed down there until the sky turned dark and the smell of other people's dinners started coming down from the flats.

And then, from somewhere above him:

"Annie! Brian! Come up and eat!"

Every child in that playground would stop and look up, and every one of them would work out whose mother it was.

"Coming!" he would shout, without moving.

"Now!"

"Coming!"

He would take his time about it, because that was the rule, and then he would go up the stairs two at a time because that was also the rule, and the flat door would be open, and there would be that particular smell of a kitchen at the end of a day, and the radio would still be going.

That was Teck Whye Lane. A hill, a block, a fourth-floor flat with a storeroom in it, three big girls next door, a playground you could be seen from, and a mother at a window who could find you anywhere in the world, because the world went about two hundred metres in every direction.

Everything that happened to Brian for the next few years happened inside it.

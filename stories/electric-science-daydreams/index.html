Upper Primary · 1994

Electric Science Daydreams

In Primary Six, Miss Lee gave the class a science project, and the wonderful, terrible thing about it was that they could do anything they liked.

"Any topic at all," she said. "As long as it is proper science. You must ask a question, and you must find the answer by testing, not by guessing."

For days Brian could think of nothing else. The trouble was that every idea he had, somebody had already done. A volcano made of baking soda and vinegar? Half the class would bring one. Bean seeds sprouting on wet cotton wool? He had grown those back in Primary Three. For a while he was quite taken with the idea of cutting worms into pieces, dropping them into jars of different solutions, and seeing which one rotted away the fastest. Nobody in the class would have thought of that. But the longer he pictured the row of jars sitting on a table in the school hall, the more he had to admit that it was a bit much, even for him. He wanted a question that was really his.

Then one evening, changing the batteries in his torch for what felt like the third time that year, he had it. Which battery lasted the longest? And was the most expensive one really the best?

Dad listened to the whole idea at the dinner table and put down his chopsticks.

"That," said Dad, "is a good question. But if you want a real answer, you must test them fairly. Same bulb, same kind of holder, every battery treated exactly the same. Otherwise it proves nothing."

So the two of them went to work. Dad helped him buy little plastic battery holders and a row of tiny bulbs, and showed him how to wire each one up so that every battery had its own bulb to light, all of them exactly alike. They lined them up on the table in the corner of the living room: Tiger Head, Eveready, Duracell, VARTA, the cheap ones and the dear ones side by side, each with its own small glowing light.

Then Brian waited.

This, it turned out, was the hard part. He had imagined the bulbs burning like little suns. They did not. They gave out a small, tired, orange sort of light, the kind you could hold your thumb in front of without feeling anything, and in the brightness of the living room in the afternoon he could hardly tell them apart at all. Was that one still going, or had it died an hour ago? He bent down until his nose was nearly touching the table. He cupped his hands round each bulb like a boy hiding a secret. He squinted from one side and then the other, and still he was guessing, and guessing was the one thing Dad had told him he was not allowed to do.

So one evening he carried the whole row, holders and wires and all, into his bedroom, got down on his knees, and pushed it carefully under his bed.

Under the bed it was properly dark. And in the dark there was nothing to guess about. A row of small orange lights, and then the same row with one gone, and then another gone, sitting there in the blackness like the windows of a very small town at night.

After that, checking became the best part of the day. He would come home from school, drop his bag, and go straight down onto the bedroom floor with his cheek almost on the tiles, one arm folded under his head, looking into the dark under the bed and counting. Then he would write the time in his notebook. He checked before dinner and after dinner. He checked before bed, and then again with his own torch under the covers when he should have been asleep, sliding out on his stomach so the springs would not creak. Some of those little bulbs burned on for days, far longer than he had ever imagined. It took him a while to work out why. The bulb in his torch was a greedy thing, big and bright and hungry, and it could flatten a pair of batteries in an evening or two. These tiny ones only sipped. Sipping like that, a single battery could keep one glowing for the better part of a week, and he began to think of them the way you think of runners in a long race, the ones you are quietly hoping for and the ones you have already given up on.

And in the end the numbers told their story. The cheapest battery died first, just as he had expected. But the longest-lasting of them all was the VARTA, and the VARTA was not the dearest on the shelf. The best battery, it turned out, was not the one that cost the most.

Brian wrote it all up, with his tables and his times and his neat little graph, and handed it in, and as far as he was concerned that was the end of it. He was not expecting anything. He had done the thing because he wanted to know the answer.

So he forgot about it. Weeks went by, and the project became just another thing he had handed up, the way homework disappears into a teacher's bag and is never thought of again.

Then, in the middle of an ordinary science lesson, Miss Lee stopped what she was doing and said that she had some news for the class. Brian's project had won second place. It took him a moment to understand that she meant him. Everybody turned round in their seats to look, and he sat there with his ears burning and his heart going hard and no idea at all what to do with his face.

He could not have told you, even that same week, what had won first place. It did not seem important then and it does not now. What he carried away was something else. Nobody had handed him the question. He had asked it himself, and he had lain on the floor in the dark night after night until he found the answer himself, and somebody had thought that was worth something.

One afternoon not long after, Brian was sitting at his desk by the window during a lesson. Outside, the clouds were doing slow and interesting things, and he was not thinking about the lesson at all. He was thinking about laboratories, and white coats, and great discoveries that nobody had ever made before, and Brian himself right in the middle of it all, a famous scientist with rows and rows of glowing bulbs.

"Brian." Miss Lee's voice cut clean across the room. "Why aren't you paying attention?"

The whole class turned to look. Brian came back from his laboratory, blinking, and found himself once again an ordinary Primary Six boy at a desk by the window, with the afternoon sun on his open book and a very long way still to go.

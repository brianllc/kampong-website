Upper Primary · 1993

Ten Cents

After Dragon Ball, Brian could not stop.

He read Detective Conan, about a boy detective who solved crimes that grown-ups could not. He read Dodge Danpei, about a small round boy who could throw a dodgeball so hard it seemed to bend the air. His best friends Weijia, Shunlai and Yehao read them too, and the comics travelled around the classroom under the desks, one book passing through four pairs of hands before the week was out.

The trouble was that four boys read faster than the world could make comics. Before long they had read everything any of them owned, twice over, and there was nothing left.

Then Yehao found the weekly.

It was a fat comic called 宝岛少年, the Chinese edition of Japan's Weekly Shonen Jump, as thick as a phone book and printed on cheap grey newsprint that left ink on your fingers. It came out every Monday, and inside it was the newest chapter of every series at once, Dragon Ball and Slam Dunk and all the rest, fresh each week long before the stories were collected into proper books.

There was only one problem. It cost two dollars.

Two dollars was a lot each week. And the worst part was that the weekly was not even a proper book you could keep. Once you had read it, it was just a thick stack of grey paper. Two dollars, gone.

"So expensive," said Shunlai. "Cannot lah."

"Wait," said Brian. "What if we share?"

They were sitting on the void deck steps after school, and Brian worked it out there on his fingers. If the four of them put in fifty cents each, that made two dollars, and two dollars bought one weekly. They would buy a single copy together and take turns with it. Each week a different boy would get to read it first, while it was still crisp and smelt of fresh ink, and the others would wait their turn.

"I'm first this week," said Yehao at once.

"Why you?" said Weijia.

"Because I found it."

Nobody could argue with that.

The system worked beautifully, until the other boys in class found out.

"Eh, can I read also?" asked Mingzhi, leaning over Brian's desk.

Brian thought about it. The weekly was already paid for. Between turns it just sat in his bag, doing nothing.

"Okay," he said slowly. "But you read after the four of us. And you pay ten cents."

Ten cents seemed fair. The four owners read it first and fresh; everyone else paid a little to read it after. By recess the next day there was a quiet queue of boys waiting to borrow the weekly for ten cents a turn, and the coins gathered in Brian's pencil case with a satisfying rattle.

By the end of the month, the four of them had read every comic they wanted, and the ten-cent coins had paid back almost everything they had spent. Their two dollars had somehow turned back into nearly two dollars, and they had read for free.

Brian thought it was the cleverest thing in the world, this trick of turning two dollars into comics and then back into two dollars again. He had no word for what they had done.

Much later, he would learn that there was a word for it. The four of them, sitting on the void deck steps with grey ink on their fingers, had started a business, their very first. But that was far too grand a thought for four boys who wanted only one thing, more than anything else in the world: to be first in line to read about a small round boy and his dodgeball.

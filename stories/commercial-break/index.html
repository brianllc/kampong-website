Kindergarten

Commercial Break

<!-- status: awaiting read · level: K · year: 1988 -->

Brian and Annie found Star Wars by accident.

It was a Sunday night in the December holidays and Annie had the television, which meant Brian did not, and he had settled on the floor behind her with no particular hopes. What came on was not a cartoon. Brian had firm views about this. Things that were not cartoons were for adults, and things for adults were boring, and he had already decided to be bored when a spaceship came across the top of the screen.

Then another one came after it.

The second one kept coming. It came and it came and it filled the whole top of the screen and it still had not finished coming, and Brian sat up off his elbows without meaning to.

"What is this?" he said.

"Shh," said Annie.

He did not move again for a long time. The story was not hard to follow at all, which surprised him nearly as much as liking it did. There was a boy called Luke who lived on a farm in the middle of a desert and wanted to be anywhere but there. There was an old man called Obi-Wan who knew a great deal more than he was saying. There was a princess called Leia who was in charge of everybody in the room including the two men who had come to rescue her, and a pilot called Han Solo who pretended not to care about any of it and cared enormously, and a tall man in black called Darth Vader who breathed like the inside of a shell.

Brian took all of them in and kept them. He was flat on his stomach within twenty minutes, chin in his hands and heels in the air, and he had not thought about being bored since the second ship.

It was during this that he became aware that he needed the toilet.

He ignored it. The film would not wait for him. He shifted his weight and pressed his heel into the floor and told himself he would go later, and the film went on and on and the needing did not go away, and by the time the picture finally cut away to an advertisement for washing powder he was in genuine difficulty.

He got up. So did Annie.

The toilet was down the little corridor past the bedrooms, ten metres at most, and they arrived at the door in a heap.

"I was first," said Annie.

"You were not."

"I got up first."

She was bigger and she was already through the door, and Brian stood outside it doing a small tight dance on the tiles and listening to the washing powder finish and a car advertisement start after it, which meant there was not much left. Annie came out. Brian went in. He was still going when he heard the music start up again in the living room, and he came back down the corridor at a run, still pulling at his shorts, and threw himself down behind his sister.

"You missed nothing," Annie said, without turning round.

That was the arrangement, then, for the rest of the night. Whoever stood up first got the door, and the second one had to be quick enough to be back before the film was. Neither of them ever suggested going during the film itself. That was not a thing you did.

At the very end the small ships went in against the enormous one, down a trench with the walls tearing past on both sides, and when Luke switched off the machine that was supposed to aim for him and aimed himself instead, Brian said "yes" out loud into the carpet. Annie did not tell him to be quiet. Annie had said something too.

The next Sunday it happened again, because the television was showing the next one.

They knew what they were doing this time. They sat down early. Brian went to the toilet before it started, on purpose, which he was rather proud of, and it made no difference at all, because it turned out you could not put an hour of needing into the bank in advance.

This one was colder and stranger than the first. It began in snow, with enormous machines that walked on four legs and could only be brought down by flying in at their ankles with a rope, which Brian considered the cleverest thing anybody had ever thought of. Then there was a swamp, and a small green creature called Yoda who put his words in the wrong order and turned out to be the one teaching Luke everything. Then there was a city in the clouds, and Han Solo was frozen into a slab and carried away, and nobody got him back.

Near the end Darth Vader said something to Luke, and Annie made a noise Brian had never heard her make before, a sort of gasp with the middle taken out of it, and both her hands went over her mouth.

"He's his father," she said through her fingers.

"I know," said Brian. "He just said so."

Annie turned right round and looked at him.

"Brian."

"What?"

She opened her mouth, and closed it again, and turned back to the television. Brian looked at the side of her face and then at the screen, where Luke was hanging off something in a great deal of distress, and he understood that something enormous had happened, and that his sister had felt it and he had not. He had heard the words perfectly well. He had followed every bit of the story that led to them. But what those words meant to Annie was a different thing altogether, and that was too complicated for Brian to work out then.

Then the picture cut to an advertisement, and the two of them were up and running before either had decided to.

Annie got there first again. She always got there first.

He had to wait a whole week to find out what happened. What he thought about during that week was not Luke, or Darth Vader, or how any of it was going to end. It was the noise his sister had made.

The third Sunday there were Ewoks in it, which Brian thought was the best thing that had happened in any of the three. By then he knew the pattern of the breaks the way you know the pattern of your own stairs, and he could feel one coming before it came.

Then, near the end, everything began happening at once, in space and in a forest and in a dark room all at the same time, and the picture cut away to an advertisement for a bank.

Brian did not move. Beside him, neither did Annie.

They sat there in the blue light of a bank advertisement, both of them absolutely still, both of them in terrible discomfort, waiting for the film to come back.

Lower Primary · ~1989 to 1991

Fire Grass

When Brian was a small boy, there were "hills" in front of his block. They were not really hills and instead were large mounds of earth grown over with grass. But to Brian and Yongjie, who were small themselves, they were proper hills, and the best place to slide down with a cardboard sled.

They knew those hills the way they knew their own homes. They knew where the ground dipped and where the lalang grew taller than their heads, where the grasshoppers hid, and where you could lie back and watch the clouds go by without anyone calling you down for dinner.

One kind of grass grew there that Brian loved best of all. It stood in long thin stalks, and at the very tip of each stalk was a cluster of tiny seeds, a deep reddish brown, so that when the late afternoon sun came slanting across the slope, the whole hillside seemed to glow.

"It looks like fire sparks," Brian said one day, holding a stalk up against the sun.

"Fire grass," said Yongjie, and the name stuck.

One afternoon there was nothing to do at all. The heat pressed down, the grasshoppers had gone quiet, and the boys lay sprawled across the slope, complaining.

"I'm bored," said Yongjie, to nobody in particular.

Brian reached up without thinking and pulled the red tip off a stalk of fire grass. The little seeds came away in his fingers, light and dry. He flicked them into the air and watched them scatter on the breeze.

Then Yongjie did the same, and soon both boys were at it, plucking the red tips and flinging the seeds as far as they could, seeing whose throw carried furthest down the slope. It was a pointless game. There was no winning it and no end to it. But it was something to do with their hands on a long, empty afternoon, and they did it again the next day, and the day after that, scattering fistfuls of red seeds across the mounds without once thinking about why.

The seeds, it turned out, had tiny barbs. By the time the boys went home each evening, the fire grass had planted itself all over them instead, stuck fast to their socks and their shorts.

Then the school holidays came, and the rain, and the boys forgot all about it.

It was Yongjie who came banging on Brian's door one morning, weeks later.

"Brian! Brian! Come and see the hill!"

Brian ran out, and stopped.

The whole hillside had turned red.

Where there had been patchy green and bare brown earth, there was now a thick, deep blanket of fire grass, every stalk tipped with red, swaying together in the wind. It ran from the foot of the slope right up to the top, and as the morning sun rose and caught it, the entire hill seemed to catch alight, glowing and rippling like a field of soft red flame.

And it was not only Brian and Yongjie. Children had come out from all the blocks around, boys and girls he knew and others he had never seen, gathered in groups, every one of them staring at the red hills that had appeared overnight as though somebody had set them alight in the dark.

Nobody knew how it had happened. The grown-ups had no idea. The other children had no idea.

Brian looked down at the seeds still stuck to his own socks, then at Yongjie. Maybe it had been them. Maybe it had not. They would never really know.

So they stood at the foot of the mounds with all the other children, heads tipped back, and watched the whole red hillside ripple and shine in the morning sun.

Author's note: What the boys called "fire grass" is really Chrysopogon aciculatus, commonly known as golden beardgrass, a slender, branching grass with reddish-brown seed heads that lit up the hillsides like little flames in the afternoon sun. Its barbed seeds cling to socks and clothes, which is exactly how it spreads, and very likely how it spread across those mounds in the first place.

Lower Primary · ~1990

The Gold Chain

About two weeks after Ah Ma's funeral, the whole family went out to the shopping centre, and there was a fair on.

It was the worst possible day to go. The fair had taken over the open space in the middle, with stalls jammed along both sides and a loudhailer going somewhere, and there were so many people that walking was not really walking. It was shuffling, and turning sideways, and being moved along by the crowd whether you wanted to go that way or not. Mum had Cherie by one hand and Brian by the other.

Brian walked the way he always walked, with his head down.

This was an old habit and nobody in the family thought anything of it any more. At the playground he was the one crouched over a crack in the concrete watching the ants come and go, or holding very still with his face close to a bush because a spider was doing something complicated with its web. On the way to school he looked at the dew sitting on the blades of grass, each drop with the whole morning bent up inside it, and at the mushrooms that had come up overnight where there had been nothing at all the day before. And he looked at what people dropped, which was a surprising amount, and most of which was rubbish, and some of which was not.

So when something shimmered at the edge of what he could see, he saw it.

It was yellow, and it lay curled in a long loose S on the floor, and for about half a second Brian's mind said snake.

Which was ridiculous. There was no snake at a shopping centre fair, in between a stall selling towels and a stall selling plastic tubs. But it had that shape, and it had a dull shine to it in among all the feet, and Brian let go of Mum's hand and pushed across two steps against the crowd to look properly.

It was a chain. A metal chain, longer than his hand, gone dark and greasy in the links, with dirt worked into it the way dirt works into a thing that has been on a floor being stepped on.

He picked it up and got back to Mum before she had finished asking where he had gone.

"What is that?"

"I found it. On the floor."

Dad took it and turned it over in the light, and Mum leaned in, and Cherie tried to get at it and was moved out of range.

"It's a chain," said Dad.

"It's a dirty chain," said Mum.

"Somebody dropped it," said Brian. "Nobody saw."

They stood there in the middle of a fair with people bumping past them on both sides, and Mum looked at it for a while longer, and then said what parents say when they do not want to make a decision in a crowd.

"Bring it home. We'll wash it. Then we'll see."

She put it in her bag. Then she added, because she was watching his face, "If it turns out to be nothing, you can have it for your science projects."

That was better than fine. That was excellent. Brian had a box of things for his science projects, and most of what was in it had come off the ground.

At home it went into a bowl with soap and hot water, and Mum worked at it with an old toothbrush, and the water went brown. The links came apart from each other and started to move properly. And what came out of the bowl at the end was not the dark greasy thing from the floor at all. It was yellow. Not bright, but yellow, the colour of a doorknob or a belt buckle.

"Brass," said Mum, drying it on a cloth. "It's brass."

Dad picked it up. He held it under the kitchen light and let it run from one hand to the other, and he did not say anything for a moment.

"Could it be gold?"

"It's brass."

"It might not be brass."

Mum looked at him. Then she looked at the chain again.

The next day she took it to the goldsmith's shop, the sort with the whole window full of chains and bangles hanging on red cards and a glass counter you could not lean on without leaving a mark. The man behind the counter took it, and looked at it, and did something to it that Brian was not there to see, and gave his verdict.

It was gold. It was very poor gold, low purity, hardly worth anything as a chain. But it was gold, and if she liked, they would take it in and melt it down and make her something else out of it.

Mum stood in the shop with the chain in her hand and thought about that for a long time.

She was thinking about a field one block from home, and a paper palace burning on a metal platform in the middle of it, and a bamboo stick put into an eight-year-old boy's hands so he could beat the ground and let his grandmother know the house had arrived. She was thinking that her son had walked into the middle of a fair with a thousand people in it, with his head down the way he always did, and had come up holding gold.

"Yes," she said. "I'd like a pendant made. A small one. For my son."

She told Brian about it afterwards, at home, in the ordinary voice she used for ordinary arrangements.

"Perhaps it was Ah Ma who left it there for you."

It took a few days. When it came back it was tiny, no bigger than a thumbnail, a little dog with its head up, because Brian was born in the year of the Dog. It had been melted and poured and worked and polished, and every bit of the fair and the floor and the feet had gone out of it, and it sat in the middle of his palm looking as though it had never been anywhere at all.

He wore it for years after that. And every so often, doing nothing in particular, he would remember what Mum had said in her ordinary voice, and he would wonder whether it really had been Ah Ma, and whether a person could do a thing like that. He never did settle it. He only knew that out of everybody at that fair, out of a thousand people, he had been the one walking with his head down.

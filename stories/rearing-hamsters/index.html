Upper Primary · ~1993

Rearing Hamsters

Zhiyuan got a hamster first.

That was how most things went. Zhiyuan would turn up with something, and Brian would look at it, and want one, and then have to go home and begin the long slow work of getting one.

He began that evening at the dinner table and he did not let it go for weeks. He would feed it himself. He would clean the cage himself. He had thought about it very carefully and it was not like the other things he had asked for, because a hamster was a responsibility, and he was ready for a responsibility.

Mum said no. Then Mum said we shall see. Then, one weekend, without any announcement at all, his parents came home with a small brown hamster in a box.

The two of them named the hamsters Chip and Dale.

There was no discussion about it. Chip and Dale were the two chipmunks out of Chip 'n Dale Rescue Rangers, which the two of them had played until they could get through it with their eyes shut, and a chipmunk was near enough a hamster, and there were two hamsters and two of them, and that settled it. Nobody stopped to notice why the names fitted so comfortably, which was that Chip and Dale are both boys. As it turned out, that was not the only thing the two of them had failed to check.

For a while, it was simply wonderful.

Brian learnt that a hamster will eat sunflower seeds the way a boy eats sweets, and that it does not eat them one at a time but stuffs them into pouches in its cheeks until its whole head goes lopsided and enormous, and then sits there looking pleased with itself, carrying more seeds than it could possibly need. He learnt that a hamster does nothing all day and then, the moment the flat goes quiet at night, gets into its wheel and runs. The wheel had a small dry squeak. Brian came to like it. It meant that in the dark, on the other side of the room, something small was busy.

He also learnt that cleaning the cage was as bad as everybody had warned him it would be. The old bedding came out damp and smelling of ammonia, and he had to do it every week, and there was no possibility of getting out of it, because he had stood at the dinner table and promised. So he did it. That was the part of having a hamster that nobody mentions and that turns out to be most of it.

Best of all were the visits.

Because if Zhiyuan had a hamster and Brian had a hamster, then obviously the hamsters were friends, in the way that two boys assume their pets must be. So Chip went to Brian's flat, and Dale went to Zhiyuan's, carried across the estate in a box on the bus, and the two of them would sit on the floor and let the hamsters out to meet each other.

They planned mazes. Of course they planned mazes. Two boys who wanted to know how things worked were not going to keep an animal without testing it, and so there were plans drawn on foolscap paper, with walls of books and cardboard, and a sunflower seed at the far end as the prize. What they discovered is what everybody discovers. A hamster has no interest whatsoever in your maze. It does not go left or right. It goes up, over the wall, and away under the cupboard, and then two boys spend the next half hour on their stomachs with a torch.

Then one week Zhiyuan kept both hamsters at his place.

It was not planned to be for long. But one week became two, and the two hamsters lived together in the one cage, and neither boy thought anything of it at all, because they were both boys and so, obviously, were the hamsters.

It was Zhiyuan who noticed that Chip was getting fat.

They discussed it seriously. Too many sunflower seeds, they decided. Not enough running. It was quite funny, really, and for a week or so they teased Chip about it and gave him slightly fewer seeds, and felt they had dealt with the matter sensibly.

Then one morning there were babies in the cage.

They had come in the night, while the whole flat was asleep. Nobody had seen it happen. Brian only heard about it when he arrived, and got down beside the cage and looked, and there they were in the bedding: pink, hairless, blind, no bigger than the top joint of his thumb, moving very slightly, like something not yet finished.

Chip, it turned out, had never been a boy at all.

The rest of that morning Brian did not entirely understand until later. There were fewer babies than there should have been. He remembers the two of them counting, and counting again, and going quiet, and Zhiyuan's face, and then the slow, sick understanding of what had happened in the cage during the night while the flat slept.

A mother hamster will sometimes eat her young. Nobody had told them this. There is no reason two boys of eleven would know it. It is the sort of fact you learn afterwards, when you are looking for an explanation for something you have already seen, and it does not help as much as you want it to.

They saved the ones that were left. They handled the cage as little as they could, and kept it dark and quiet, and left the mother alone with them, and the ones that survived that first night grew fur and opened their eyes and turned into proper hamsters, small and fast and endlessly hungry.

And then, of course, they had to give them all away. There was no space, and there was no permission, and there had never been any question of a flat holding that many hamsters. So the babies went off one by one to other people, and Chip and Dale went back to being one hamster in one flat and one hamster in another.

They never put the two of them together again after that. Not for a fortnight, not for a week, not for a single night. Neither boy needed it explained to him, and neither of them argued about it, because it was the kind of mistake you only have to make once, and neither of them ever wanted to kneel down in front of that cage and count again.

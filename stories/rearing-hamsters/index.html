Upper Primary · 1994

Rearing Hamsters

Zhiyuan got a hamster first.

That was how most things went. Zhiyuan would turn up with something, and Brian would look at it, and want one, and then go home and begin the long slow work of getting one.

He started that same evening at the dinner table.

"I will feed it myself," he said. "Every day. And I will clean the cage."

"You said that about the fish," said Mum.

"The fish was different. The fish did not do anything."

"Hamsters bite," said Mum.

"Only if you frighten them."

Dad said nothing at all and went on eating, which Brian had learnt was not a no.

He kept it up for three weeks. Then one Saturday his parents came home without saying anything about it beforehand, and Dad put a small cardboard box on the table with holes punched in the lid, and inside it, sitting in a corner and looking extremely unimpressed, was a small brown hamster.

They named the hamsters Chip and Dale.

"Chip and Dale," said Zhiyuan, when they had them side by side on the floor. "Like the game."

"Which one is Chip?"

"Yours can be Dale," said Zhiyuan, "because he is fatter."

Neither of them stopped to think about why two names from Chip 'n Dale Rescue Rangers fitted a pair of hamsters quite so comfortably.

For a while, it was simply wonderful.

A hamster, Brian discovered, eats sunflower seeds the way a boy eats sweets, and does not eat them one at a time but packs them into pouches in its cheeks until its whole head goes lopsided and enormous, and then sits there looking pleased with itself carrying more seeds than it could possibly need. A hamster also does nothing whatsoever all day, and then, the moment the flat goes quiet at night, gets into its wheel and runs. The wheel had a small dry squeak, and Brian came to like it. It meant that in the dark, on the other side of the room, something small was busy.

Cleaning the cage was every bit as bad as he had been warned. The old bedding came out damp and sharp-smelling, and it had to be done every week, and there was no getting out of it.

"That cage smells," said Mum from the kitchen door, one Sunday.

"I am doing it now," said Brian.

"You said that yesterday."

"I am doing it now," said Brian, doing it now.

Best of all were the visits.

If Zhiyuan had a hamster and Brian had a hamster, then obviously the hamsters were friends and would want to see each other. So Chip came over to Teck Whye in a box, and Dale went the other way, and the two boys would sit on the floor with the hamsters out between them.

Naturally, they planned a maze. Two boys who wanted to know how things worked were not going to keep an animal without testing it.

"Walls made of books," said Zhiyuan, drawing it out on foolscap paper. "Here, and here. Then he must turn."

"And a sunflower seed at the end," said Brian. "So he knows where to go."

"How long you think he will take?"

"One minute."

"Half a minute," said Zhiyuan.

They drew several versions of it, each one grander than the last, and argued about whether it counted if you helped him at a corner. They never built a single one of them. Somehow there was always a better afternoon for it, and then the hamsters were out of the cage and running about the floor and being much more interesting than a plan on a piece of paper.

Then one week Zhiyuan kept both hamsters at his place.

It was not meant to be for long. But one week became two, and the two of them lived together in the one cage, and neither boy thought a thing about it, because they were both boys and so, obviously, were the hamsters.

It was Zhiyuan who noticed first.

"Eh," he said. "Chip is getting very fat."

"You are feeding him too much."

"I feed both the same."

"Then he is lazy."

They agreed it was the seeds and the lack of running, and for a week or so they teased Chip about it and gave him slightly less, and felt they had dealt with the matter sensibly.

Then one morning there were babies in the cage.

They had come in the night while the whole flat was asleep, and nobody had seen it happen. Brian only heard when he arrived, and he got down beside the cage and looked, and there they were in the bedding: pink, hairless, blind, no bigger than the top joint of his thumb, moving very slightly, like something not yet finished.

Chip, it turned out, had never been a boy at all.

"How many?" said Brian.

Zhiyuan counted. Then he counted again, more slowly.

Then he did not say anything, and Brian looked at his face and then looked back into the cage and understood, in a slow and horrible way, that there were fewer than there had been in the night.

A mother hamster will sometimes eat her young. Nobody had told them this. There was no reason two boys of eleven would know it, and it is the sort of fact you only go looking for afterwards, when you already have something you need explained, and it does not help nearly as much as you want it to.

They saved the ones that were left. They handled the cage as little as they could and kept it dark and quiet and left the mother alone with them, and the ones that came through that first night grew fur and opened their eyes and turned into proper hamsters, small and fast and endlessly hungry.

And then, of course, they had to give them all away. There was no space and no permission and there had never been any question of a flat holding that many hamsters. So the babies went off one by one to other people, and Chip and Dale went back to being one hamster in one flat and one hamster in another.

They never put the two of them together again after that. Not for a fortnight, not for a week, not for a single night. Neither boy needed it explained to him and neither of them argued about it, because it was the kind of mistake you only have to make once, and neither of them ever wanted to kneel down in front of that cage and count again.

Kindergarten · ~1988

True or False

A bag of marbles cost fifty cents at the mama shop.

They were glass, and inside each one was a curl of colour, a little twist of blue or green or red suspended in the middle like something frozen. Held up to the light they were the best thing Brian owned. A few of them were special, with a metallic shine to them, and those ones nobody played with. Those got kept.

In the evenings the boys fought over them on the long dirt patch beside the playground.

The game went like this. Somebody scratched a circle in the dirt with a stick, and everybody put marbles into it. Then they paced out five or six metres, sometimes eight if they were feeling grand, and scratched a second line, and that was where you stood. Any marble you knocked out of the circle was yours to keep. Only the first throw came from the line. After that you threw from wherever your own marble had come to rest, so a bad throw left you stranded far away and a good one left you crouching right up close with the whole circle in front of you.

To knock one out on your very first throw, all the way from the line, was the finest thing a boy could do. It happened to Brian perhaps twice in his life.

And the circle was not the only thing to worry about. Once your own marble was lying out there in the dirt, it could be hit as well, and whoever hit it took it.

So no two boys played the same way. Yongjie went for the circle and nothing but the circle, throwing long and hard every single turn.

"Wah, so greedy," said Guowei, who never did anything of the sort. Guowei stayed wide of the crowd and crept up in small safe throws and was very hard to catch out.

"You throw like my grandmother," said Yongjie.

"My grandmother still got her marbles," said Guowei.

On the good evenings there were eight or ten of them out there in the last of the light, arguing about whether a marble had really crossed the line, until somebody's mother leaned out of a flat upstairs and hollered a name.

But on this particular evening there was nobody on the dirt patch at all except Brian and an older boy named Zhikai.

"Cannot play," said Zhikai, looking at the empty patch. "Two people only."

Brian knew he was right. You need a crowd for the circle game.

"We play the other way," said Zhikai. "You stand there, I stand here. Take turns. First one to hit the other fellow's marble wins."

That sounded fair enough. It was only the ordinary rule, Brian thought, with the circle taken away.

"Okay," said Brian.

"True or False?"

Brian looked at him.

He knew what true meant. True meant yes. He did not know what false meant. Nobody had ever asked him this question before in his life, and there was an older boy standing there in the dirt waiting for an answer, so he said the word he knew.

"True," said Brian.

"Sure?"

"True."

"Okay," said Zhikai.

They played. Brian had begun, as he always did, with his favourite marble, the one he liked best of everything he owned.

Zhikai hit it on his fourth throw.

He walked over without hurrying, picked the marble up out of the dirt, blew the dust off it, and put it in his pocket.

"True means real one," he said, not unkindly. "Real battle. Lose already, the marble is mine. That's why I asked you. False means play only."

Brian stood there and did not say anything, because he had been asked and he had answered, and everybody plays by what they said.

He went home without it.

Marbles came and went after that. Over the months that followed he won some and he lost some, and somewhere in there he found another one he loved just as much, a new favourite with a perfect curve that shimmered a certain way when he turned it.

But he was a good deal more careful, ever afterwards, about saying yes to a question he did not understand.

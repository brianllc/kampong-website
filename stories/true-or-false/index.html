Kindergarten · ~1988

True or False

A bag of marbles cost fifty cents at the mama shop.

They were glass, and inside each one was a curl of colour, a little twist of blue or green or red suspended in the middle like something frozen. Held up to the light they were the best thing Brian owned. A few of the ones you got were special. Those had a metallic shine to them, and you did not play those. You kept those.

The boys traded them constantly. But mostly, in the evening, they fought over them on the long dirt patch beside the playground.

The game went like this.

You drew a circle in the dirt, and everybody put some of their marbles inside it. Then you paced out five or six metres, sometimes eight if you were feeling grand, and drew a second line, and that was where you stood. Any marble you could knock out of the circle was yours to keep. Only the first throw had to come from the line. After that you threw from wherever your own marble had come to rest, which meant a bad throw left you far away and a good one left you crouching close with the whole circle in front of you.

To knock one out on your very first throw, all the way from the line, was the finest thing a boy could do. It happened to Brian perhaps twice in his life. It was possible he was not skilful. It was possible he was only lucky. He was never in a hurry to work out which.

On the good evenings there were ten or twelve of them out there in the last of the light, arguing about whether a marble had really crossed the line, until somebody's mother called down from a corridor upstairs.

But on this particular evening there was nobody there except Brian and an older boy.

Two is not enough for the circle game. You need a crowd for that. So the older boy thought about it and said they would do it the other way instead. They would start at opposite ends of the dirt patch, and take turns, and the first one to hit the other boy's marble would win.

Brian said all right.

Then the older boy said, "True or False?"

Brian looked at him.

He knew what true meant. True meant yes. True meant the proper way, the real way, the way you did things when you were serious about them. He did not know what false meant. He had never been asked this question before in his life and there was another boy standing there waiting for his answer, so he said the word that sounded better.

"True," said Brian.

They played. Brian had begun, as he always did, with his favourite marble, the one he liked best of everything he had.

The older boy hit it.

And that was when Brian learnt what he had agreed to, because True, it turned out, meant a real battle, and in a real battle the loser hands his marble over. He watched the older boy pick it up out of the dirt and put it in his pocket, and there was nothing to say, because he had been asked and he had answered and everybody had heard him.

He went home without it.

Marbles came and went after that. Over the months that followed he won some and he lost some, the way you do, and somewhere in there he found another one he loved just as much, a new favourite to carry about in the bottom of the bag and take out and hold up to the light.

But he was a good deal more careful, ever afterwards, about saying yes to a question he did not understand.

Upper Primary · ~1993

Firepowder

Zhiyuan was the cleverest boy Brian knew.

He lived over in the Bukit Panjang estate, and Brian lived in Teck Whye, and in between there was just enough distance that meeting up had to be planned. It was always worth planning. Other boys in the class were funny, or fast, or good at football. Zhiyuan wanted to know how things worked, and that was a different thing altogether, and it turned out that Brian wanted the same. Between them they had taken apart a radio, a torch and one of Mum's kitchen timers, and had put back together slightly fewer things than they had opened.

So when Chinese New Year came round and the shops filled up with things that burned, the two of them were interested for reasons of their own.

There were pop pop crackers, little twists of paper you threw hard at the ground so they cracked like a whip and made your auntie jump. There were the long ones on sticks, thin paper tubes packed tight with powder, which you lit at one end and held up while they hissed and threw out a fountain of white fire. Every boy in the neighbourhood had them. In the evenings the playground flickered.

Most boys lit them, waved them about, and that was that.

Zhiyuan sat on the step with an unlit one in his hand, turning it over.

"You know what's inside here?" he said.

"Fire," said Brian.

"Powder," said Zhiyuan. "Gunpowder. The whole stick is just a paper tube with powder inside. And we are burning it one spoon at a time, waving it in the air like small kids."

Brian looked at the stick in his own hand. He had held a hundred of these and had never once thought of it that way.

"So?" he said.

"So," said Zhiyuan, "what happens if we save it all up?"

They began to harvest that evening.

It was slow, fiddly work. Sitting on the cool floor of a staircase landing, they split each paper tube down its length with a pair of scissors, eased it open, and tipped the contents out. Inside was a dull grey powder, fine as sand, with a sharp smell that stayed on their fingers long after they had washed them. One stick gave a disappointing little heap. Ten sticks gave something you could begin to take seriously.

"Careful, don't spill," said Zhiyuan, about forty times.

They took apart every stick they were given, thirty or thereabouts, and poured it all into a glass jar, and by the end they had the jar a quarter full.

Then they went looking for somewhere to use it.

They chose a field in Teck Whye, a good long way from the blocks, out where the grass came up past their shins and nobody could see what two boys were doing. There they dug a hole, scooping the earth out with a broken plastic container, and poured the whole jar into it.

Then came the fuse, and the fuse was the part they had thought about least, because both of them had seen it done a hundred times in cartoons and had assumed it simply worked. In a cartoon the fuse runs along the ground in a beautiful fizzing line, and the man who lit it has all the time in the world to walk away and put his fingers in his ears. So they laid a long piece of string from the powder out across the grass, exactly like that, and crouched down at the far end of it, and lit it.

The string caught. It burned. It travelled about as far as a hand span, and went out.

They looked at each other.

"Maybe the match not long enough," said Brian.

They tried again, holding the match on until it nearly burnt their fingers. It went out. They tried a different piece of string. It went out. They tried a shorter length, which felt like cheating, and it went out anyway, and by then the sun was getting low and they were both lying on their stomachs in the grass beside a quarter jar of gunpowder in a hole and a row of small dead pieces of string.

"The string cannot carry the fire by itself," said Zhiyuan at last.

Brian thought about that.

"Then we give it a little boost," he said.

So they coated a string in the powder itself. They rolled it and dusted it and worked the grey grains down into the fibres along its whole length, until the string was not white any more but grey from end to end.

"Enough or not?" said Brian.

"More," said Zhiyuan.

They laid it out again, and got down low, and lit it.

This time it did not go out.

The fire ran along the ground towards the hole with a bright hissing purpose, and the two of them watched it go and did not dare to breathe.

Then the field went off.

It was not the enormous blast of the cartoons. It was a small boom, flat and solid and over almost before it had begun, and a scoop of loose earth jumped up out of the hole. But it was much louder than either of them had expected, loud enough that Brian was startled, and somewhere a long way off a bird went up out of a tree.

And above the hole, the smoke climbed. It went up in a thin grey column and opened out at the top into a small round cap, a tiny mushroom cloud, exactly like the ones in the encyclopaedia, hanging there over a field in Teck Whye for a few seconds before the wind took it apart.

"Zhiyuan," said Brian. "Zhiyuan, look."

But Zhiyuan was already looking, and then the two of them were laughing so hard in the grass that neither could get a word out, because it had worked, and because a quarter of a glass jar of powder from a few Chinese New Year sticks had just made a mushroom cloud of its very own.

They filled in the hole. They walked home and said nothing to anybody about it.

And in all the years afterwards, Brian never stopped being quietly amazed that not one single neighbour, not one auntie at a window, not one uncle downstairs, ever thought it worth finding out what that noise had been.
